mistakes are expected, respected, and inspected. Each entry names the student behavior,
gives the teacher a move in a real human voice, and cites the source that grounds it.

toward roughly three entries as we add them.

## 6.RP.A.1 — Ratio language

> Understand the concept of a ratio and use ratio language to describe a ratio
> relationship between two quantities. *For example, "The ratio of wings to beaks in the
> bird house at the zoo was 2:1, because for every 2 wings there was 1 beak."*

`case_uuid: 5268d3ab-91f6-565e-a7e3-a18ef8a06836`

### Reading a part-to-part ratio as a part-to-whole

**Student behavior.** The student writes or reads 2 to 3 as "2 out of 5," treating a
comparison of two separate groups as one group inside a whole (or the reverse). The
numbers are right; what the ratio points at is not.

**Teacher move.** This is usually a definition question, not a reasoning gap, so it is
a good moment to go back to the text. A ratio of 2 to 3 means for every 2 of the first
thing there are 3 of the second, the same way the standard says it: for every 2 wings
there was 1 beak. Ask the student to read our example, then lay their problem next to
it. Does their 2 to 3 describe two groups compared to each other, or one group sitting
inside a whole? Let them place it. Going back to the definition is always a safe move,
and it teaches them to use the text instead of rederiving it every time.

*Grounds:* Illustrative Mathematics grade 6 ratio task (part-to-part vs part-to-whole),
CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, cite and link only. Standard text and example: CCSS-M / California
Department of Education, public.

### Mistaking the scale factor for the final count

**Student behavior.** In a 4 to 5 ratio of boys to girls with 270 students, the student
finds the scale factor correctly, 270 divided by 9 parts is 30, and then reports 30 as
the number of boys instead of 4 times 30, which is 120.

**Teacher move.** The 30 is right, so do not erase it, start there. Ask what the 30
counts. They split 270 into 9 equal parts, so 30 is the size of one part, one share. Then
ask whether the boys are one share or more than one. They are 4 of them. Once the student
sees 30 as one part and the boys as four of those parts, 4 times 30 is 120, and checking
the girls at 5 times 30 is 150 brings them back to 270. The move is naming what the number
measures before spending it: 30 is a share, not the boys.

*Grounds:* Illustrative Mathematics, "Evaluating Ratio Statements" (6.RP.A.1), CC
BY-NC-SA 4.0, cite and link only. Verified live: the task builds "there are exactly 30
boys" in as a false statement because 30 is the scale factor, not the count.

## 6.RP.A.2 — Unit rate

> Understand the concept of a unit rate a/b associated with a ratio a:b with b ≠ 0, and
> use rate language in the context of a ratio relationship. *For example, "This recipe
> has a ratio of 3 cups of flour to 4 cups of sugar, so there is 3/4 cup of flour for
> each cup of sugar."*

`case_uuid: 703d2a83-42ad-50b3-aaae-1475dba3f147`

### Finding the rate the wrong way up

**Student behavior.** The student divides in the wrong direction and reports the
reciprocal: hours per mile when the question wants miles per hour, dollars-per-item
flipped to items-per-dollar. The division itself is done correctly.

**Teacher move.** Nine times out of ten this is an organizing slip, not a
misunderstanding of rate, so do not send them back to recompute. Set the problem up
first: write what we have with its units, and write the answer we want with its units.
If the question asks for miles per hour, the answer is going to read as miles over
hours. Line the numbers up against that and the flip shows itself. Unit analysis catches
this faster than any recalculation, and it is a habit that keeps catching it.

*Grounds:* Achieve the Core, Ratios and Rates Mini-Assessment (grade 6, cluster
6.RP.A), CC0. Reversal as a documented rate error: Çalışıcı (2018), CC BY 4.0.

### Subtracting instead of dividing to get the unit rate

**Student behavior.** To find a unit rate the student subtracts rather than divides.
Given $6 for 3 pounds, they write 6 minus 3 equals 3 instead of 6 divided by 3
equals 2.

**Teacher move.** Rather than mark it wrong, hand the reasoning back. Use their own
numbers: $6 for 3 pounds. Ask what the 6 minus 3 is measuring. It gives 3, but 3 of
what? It is not a price. Now ask what one pound costs. Six dollars shared over 3 pounds
is $2 each, and $2 is a price. Once they see that subtracting lands on 3 with no name
and dividing lands on a real price, they have inspected their own step and found the fix
themselves. That sits better than being told the operation was wrong.

*Grounds:* Achieve the Core, Ratios and Rates Mini-Assessment (grade 6, cluster
6.RP.A), CC0. Additive-for-multiplicative reasoning: Çalışıcı (2018), CC BY 4.0.

## 6.RP.A.3 — Ratio and rate reasoning

> Use ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world and mathematical problems, e.g., by
> reasoning about tables of equivalent ratios, tape diagrams, double number line
> diagrams, or equations.

`case_uuid: 91413510-cc32-589d-a3e5-274b1a3fc339`

### Adding across a ratio table instead of scaling

**Student behavior.** The student extends a ratio by adding rather than multiplying. In
a table where 2 maps to 6, they get 4 maps to 8 by adding 2, instead of 4 maps to 12 by
scaling times three. The addition is correct; the move is not.

**Teacher move.** Before anything, get a rough answer or a range. If 2 gets us 6, then
4, which is double, should get us something near double 6, not 8. That estimate is the
whole tell. Notice the arithmetic is fine: 4 plus 4 is 8, run correctly. The wrong turn
is the step they chose, not the step they ran, so retrace which move they made rather
than checking their addition. A quick diagram or a double number line makes the scaling
visible. Then hold back. Do not point at the gap between 8 and 12. Ask for the estimate,
let them set their 8 next to it, and let them find it.

*Grounds:* Achieve the Core, Ratios and Rates Mini-Assessment (grade 6, cluster
6.RP.A), CC0. Additive vs multiplicative reasoning as a documented misconception:
Çalışıcı (2018), CC BY 4.0.
