# Guideline 3: Building Knowledge

## Summary

Guideline 3 sits in [[multiple-means-of-representation]], the **Representation**
column of CAST's 3×3 grid, in the **Executive Function** row, the same row position
as Emotional Capacity (Engagement) and Strategy Development (Action & Expression). See
[[access-support-executive-function]] for the full grid. CAST's guiding question: "How
might we incorporate multiple ways for building individual and collective knowledge?"
It contains four considerations: [[connect-prior-knowledge]] (3.1),
[[patterns-critical-features-and-big-ideas]] (3.2), [[multiple-ways-of-knowing]] (3.3),
and [[transfer-and-generalization]] (3.4).

**This guideline was called Comprehension in UDL 2.2.** If you trained on the older
framework, this is the guideline you're looking for. The rename is one of five
guideline-name changes in the whole 3.0 revision (the others being Physical Action →
Interaction, Executive Functions → Strategy Development, Recruiting Interest →
Welcoming Interests & Identities, and Self-Regulation → Emotional Capacity). CAST's
terminology mapping confirms the change; the staged sources don't give a
guideline-specific rationale beyond the general 3.0 language shift away from passive,
educator-centered framing. It's worth noting, though, that the new name fits the
guideline's own content: the description leads with "building usable knowledge...
depends not upon merely perceiving information, but upon active skills," which reads
as a direct argument against "comprehension" as a passive-receipt frame, but that
connection is this page's reading, not a stated CAST rationale.

## When to reach for it

Reach for Guideline 3 when a learner perceives the content and understands its
vocabulary and symbols, but still isn't turning it into knowledge they can use,
organize, or apply elsewhere. This is the guideline for what happens *after* perception
and decoding succeed.

| The actual problem | Go to |
|---|---|
| The learner lacks, or doesn't recognize the relevance of, the background knowledge this new material depends on | [[connect-prior-knowledge]] (3.1) |
| The learner takes in the material but can't tell what's central versus what's peripheral | [[patterns-critical-features-and-big-ideas]] (3.2) |
| The instructional approach itself privileges one way of knowing (e.g., linear, individual, Western-academic) and excludes others that would work for some learners | [[multiple-ways-of-knowing]] (3.3) |
| The learner grasps the material in the context it was taught but can't apply or recognize it in a new context | [[transfer-and-generalization]] (3.4) |
| The learner can't perceive the content's channel at all | [[perception]] (Guideline 1) |
| The barrier is the vocabulary, symbols, or notation itself, before knowledge-building even starts | [[language-and-symbols]] (Guideline 2) |
| The barrier is goal-setting, planning, or monitoring progress on a task, not building knowledge from content | [[strategy-development]] (Guideline 6) |

3.2 and 3.4 are closely related and worth distinguishing precisely: 3.2 is about
recognizing what matters *within* the material being taught right now. 3.4 is about
carrying what was learned *out* of that material into a different context later. A
learner can ace 3.2 (spot the big idea in this unit) and still fail 3.4 (not recognize
that same idea when it shows up in a different subject next month).

## How it works

CAST's definition names knowledge-building as active work, not passive intake:

> Building usable knowledge requires "making connections, synthesizing information,
> asking questions, selective attending, integrating new information with prior
> knowledge, strategic categorization, and active memorization."

The source adds that this is not solely an individual process either: "collaborative
environments where students engage in shared inquiry and problem-solving foster deeper
understanding and collective knowledge advancement." That collective framing is a
throughline across all four considerations: 3.1 through 3.4 are usually described in
individual cognitive terms (connecting, highlighting, transferring), but 3.3
specifically widens the lens to knowledge-building as something done in community and
through more than one epistemology, including holistic and Indigenous ways of
organizing understanding alongside more familiar linear/Western academic approaches.

**A gloss worth flagging.** The staged extract for this guideline attaches specific
cognitive-science labels (schema theory, cognitive load theory, transfer) to several
considerations. Those labels are useful shorthand, but they are the staging research's
own analytical gloss layered onto CAST's design options, not phrases CAST's guideline
text itself uses. Treat "this is schema theory" as a reasonable interpretive frame, not
a CAST citation.

## In practice

The four considerations, in CAST's own one-line framing:

- **3.1**: Build connections to prior understandings and experiences.
- **3.2**: Accentuate important information and how it relates to the learning goal.
- **3.3**: Honor multiple and diverse ways of developing understandings of the world.
- **3.4**: Apply learning to new contexts.

Each has its own design-options list and worked example on its own page.

**A worked pass.** A U.S. history unit covers the causes of a 20th-century economic
crisis through a dense chronological narrative: dates, policies, names, in sequence,
with no other structure. Three different students hit three different walls:

- One has never learned what a "market" or "supply and demand" even means outside this
  unit: there's no scaffold connecting this new material to anything he already
  understands. That's 3.1: he needs the prior-knowledge bridge built before the new
  content can land.
- One reads and understands every sentence but comes away with forty equally-weighted
  facts and no sense of which three or four actually explain the crisis. That's 3.2:
  she needs the critical features highlighted, not more content.
- One aces the unit test on this crisis specifically, then fails to recognize the same
  causal pattern (asset bubble, overleveraging, policy delay) when it appears in a
  different historical crisis two units later. That's 3.4: the knowledge was built but
  never explicitly generalized past its original context.

A single narrative-heavy unit like this one is a common site for all three barriers at
once, which is part of why Guideline 3 has four separate considerations rather than one.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Do not write "Comprehension" as the current name of this guideline anywhere.** It
  is 2.2 vocabulary. Building Knowledge is correct for 3.0 and is what this wiki uses
  throughout.
- **The Executive Function row placement is not obviously intuitive for this
  guideline.** "Executive function" more naturally evokes planning and self-monitoring
  (which is where Strategy Development, Guideline 6, actually lives) than it does
  knowledge construction. The row groups guidelines by their functional role in
  CAST's architecture, not by surface-level word association with "executive." See
  [[access-support-executive-function]] for how the grid's three rows are actually
  defined.
- **The cognitive-science vocabulary in the staged source is gloss, not CAST
  citation**, as noted above. Don't present "schema theory" or "cognitive load theory"
  on any Guideline 3 page as if CAST's own guideline text uses those terms. It
  doesn't, in the extract available here.
- **3.3's inclusion of Indigenous and non-Western epistemologies is a 3.0 addition in
  spirit,** consistent with the version's broader asset-based, sociocultural framing.
  Whether 3.3 existed in some other form under 2.2's Comprehension guideline is not
  something the staged sources establish: the version-history source explicitly flags
  checkpoint-level mappings between 2.2 and 3.0 as unconfirmed. Treat this
  consideration as unmapped territory rather than assuming either a clean carryover or
  a clean addition.
