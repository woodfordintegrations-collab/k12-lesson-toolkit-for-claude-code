# Guideline 2: Language & Symbols

## Summary

Guideline 2 sits in [[multiple-means-of-representation]], the **Representation**
column of CAST's 3×3 grid, in the **Support** row, the same row position as
Sustaining Effort & Persistence (Engagement) and Expression & Communication (Action &
Expression). See [[access-support-executive-function]] for the full grid. CAST's
guiding question: "How might we offer, honor, and value multiple representations of
language and symbols?" It contains five considerations, the most of any guideline in
this principle: [[clarify-vocabulary-and-language-structures]] (2.1),
[[decoding-text-notation-and-symbols]] (2.2),
[[understanding-across-languages-and-dialects]] (2.3),
[[biases-in-language-and-symbols]] (2.4), and [[illustrate-through-multiple-media]] (2.5).

## When to reach for it

Reach for Guideline 2 when a learner can perceive the information just fine (they can
see the text, hear the audio) but the linguistic or symbolic code it's written in is
what's stopping them.

| The actual problem | Go to |
|---|---|
| Specific words, symbols, or unfamiliar sentence/notation structures are the barrier to meaning | [[clarify-vocabulary-and-language-structures]] (2.1) |
| The learner struggles with the mechanics of decoding the code itself (sounding out text, parsing math notation), not the meaning once decoded | [[decoding-text-notation-and-symbols]] (2.2) |
| The learner speaks a different home language or dialect and needs it supported and valued, not just translated around | [[understanding-across-languages-and-dialects]] (2.3) |
| The problem is a language hierarchy: standard English treated as the only legitimate form, other dialects stigmatized | [[biases-in-language-and-symbols]] (2.4) |
| The material leans on text as its only mode and a non-text illustration would carry the concept better for everyone | [[illustrate-through-multiple-media]] (2.5) |
| The learner can't perceive the content's channel at all, before language even enters it | [[perception]] (Guideline 1) |
| The words and symbols are understood, but the learner isn't building connected knowledge from them | [[building-knowledge]] (Guideline 3) |

Two pairs inside this guideline are easy to collapse into each other and shouldn't be:

- **2.1 vs. 2.2**: 2.1 is about *meaning*: does the learner know what this word,
  symbol, or structure means. 2.2 is about *decoding*: can the learner turn the marks
  on the page into that word or symbol in the first place, fluently enough not to
  drown in cognitive load doing it. A learner can decode a sentence perfectly and still
  not know what "photosynthesis" means (2.1); a learner can know exactly what a word
  means and still not be able to sound it out (2.2).
- **2.3 vs. 2.4**: 2.3 is a support move: make key information available in a
  learner's home or heritage language, so multilingual learners aren't shut out. 2.4 is
  a bias move: dismantle the assumption that standard English (or any single dialect)
  is the correct or superior form and others are deficient. You can do 2.3 without
  doing 2.4, and a classroom that translates everything into Spanish while still
  treating AAVE as "incorrect" has done one without the other.

## How it works

CAST's official framing states the core problem directly:

> "Inequalities arise when information is presented to all learners through a single
> form of representation." Multiple representations should be available "not only for
> accessibility, but for clarity, comprehensibility, and creating a shared
> understanding for all learners."

That's a wider claim than accessibility alone: Guideline 2 argues single-representation
language creates barriers for learners who could technically perceive the content
(passing Guideline 1) but still can't extract meaning from the specific linguistic form
it's locked into. The guideline explicitly names multilingual learners and dialect
speakers as a core audience, not an edge case bolted onto a guideline really meant for
something else: three of its five considerations (2.1's embedded translations, 2.3,
and 2.4) are built around linguistic and dialectal diversity directly.

## In practice

The five considerations, in CAST's own one-line framing:

- **2.1**: Clarify vocabulary, symbols, and language structures for all learners.
- **2.2**: Support decoding of text, math notation, and symbols.
- **2.3**: Cultivate understanding and respect across languages and dialects.
- **2.4**: Address biases present in language and symbol use.
- **2.5**: Illustrate concepts through multiple media formats.

Each has its own design-options list and worked example on its own page.

**A worked pass.** An algebra word problem reads: "A vendor's marginal revenue
decreases by a constant rate as quantity sold increases beyond the equilibrium point."
Three students are stuck, for three different reasons:

- One knows algebra cold but doesn't know what "marginal revenue" or "equilibrium
  point" mean as terms: that's 2.1, and the fix is linking the vocabulary to a
  definition or a simpler restatement, not reteaching algebra.
- One is a recent immigrant whose math skills are strong in his home language but who
  is still building English proficiency: that's 2.3, and the fix might be making the
  key terms available bilingually rather than assuming full English fluency is a
  prerequisite for demonstrating the math.
- One understands every word but the sentence's syntax (a long conditional clause
  before the subject) is what's tripping comprehension. That's still 2.1 (unfamiliar
  language *structure*, not vocabulary), and the fix is restating the relationship
  explicitly rather than assuming the syntax is transparent.

None of these three students has a decoding problem (2.2) or a bias problem (2.4); a
generic "make this more accessible" response that reached for read-aloud text-to-speech
(a 2.2 tool) would help none of them.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **2.5 overlaps with 1.2 but isn't the same lens.** [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]]
  (1.2) is about whether an alternative *perceptual channel* exists for learners who
  can't use the primary one at all. 2.5 is about whether text-only representation is
  limiting comprehension for everyone, independent of a perceptual disability: the
  staged source frames it as a general design habit ("relying on text as a single mode
  of representation can impose barriers") more than an access accommodation. A lesson
  can need 2.5 with no student in it having any perceptual barrier at all.
- **This guideline is not primarily an English-Language-Learner accommodation
  guideline**, even though 2.3 and 2.4 center multilingual learners heavily. The
  considerations apply to any learner whose barrier is linguistic or symbolic form,
  including monolingual English speakers stuck on jargon or notation.
- **The source doesn't give 2.3 and 2.4 a bright dividing line** beyond the
  support-vs-bias framing above; the staged extract's own language for both leans on
  the same "hierarchy of language" material, so some judgment is required to place a
  given classroom situation in one or the other. Where a situation is genuinely both,
  it likely needs both considerations, not a forced choice.
