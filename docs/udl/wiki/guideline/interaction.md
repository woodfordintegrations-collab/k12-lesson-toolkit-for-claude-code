# Guideline 4: Interaction

## Summary

Guideline 4 sits in [[multiple-means-of-action-and-expression]], the **Action &
Expression** column of CAST's 3×3 grid, in the **Access** row, the same row position
as Perception (Representation) and Welcoming Interests & Identities (Engagement). See
[[access-support-executive-function]] for the full grid. CAST's guiding question: "How
might we design materials and physical environments that ensure access and
participation for every learner?" It contains two considerations:
[[response-navigation-and-movement]] (4.1) and [[access-to-assistive-technologies]]
(4.2).

**This guideline was called "Physical Action" in UDL 2.2.** That name is retired; do
not use it as current, though readers trained on 2.2 will still search for it.

## When to reach for it

Reach for Guideline 4 when the barrier is in the **physical or motor act** of engaging
with material or environment: not in perceiving it, understanding it, choosing how to
express it, or planning the work.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| They cannot perceive the material at all, regardless of how they'd interact with it | [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] (1.2) |
| The design doesn't yet offer more than one way to respond, navigate, or move: one motor pathway only | [[response-navigation-and-movement]] (4.1) |
| Flexible methods exist in the design, but *this learner's* actual assistive technology, device, or training isn't there to use them | [[access-to-assistive-technologies]] (4.2) |
| They can physically operate every input method, but the medium of their output is the obstacle | [[multiple-media-for-communication]] (5.1) |
| The barrier is in planning, organizing, or monitoring the task, not in touching or moving through it | [[strategy-development]] (Guideline 6) |
| You need to know whether a design choice satisfies a legal accessibility obligation | not this guideline; see [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]] |

The diagnostic question: "**is the barrier in the motor act of navigating or
responding, separate from what's being communicated or how the task is planned?**"
CAST's own framing draws this line: it names "page turning, mouse/keyboard use" as the
kind of restriction that creates barriers for learners with physical disabilities,
blindness, dysgraphia, or executive-functioning support needs: a different problem
from Guideline 5's, which is about which *medium* a learner produces, not whether they
can operate the interface at all.

## How it works

CAST's guideline-level description: "Design materials and physical environments that
ensure access and participation for every learner." It states that "intentionally
designed curricular materials provide a seamless interface with common assistive and
accessible technologies," the goal is not an add-on for a subset of learners but a
default that lets assistive technology work without friction.

The two considerations split the problem cleanly:

- **4.1** is about the *design of the interaction itself*: building in more than one
  motor pathway (mouse, keyboard, switch, voice, touch) and flexible timing, so no
  single physical requirement is load-bearing.
- **4.2** is about *access to the tools that use those pathways*: the accessible
  materials and assistive technologies themselves, plus a point CAST makes explicitly:
  providing tools alone is insufficient; learners require support in using them
  effectively. A design can pass 4.1 in the abstract and still fail a learner who has
  the right assistive technology but was never shown how to use it with this material.

**This is where UDL meets enforceable law, and the two are not the same thing.** UDL is
a design framework with no success criteria of its own: CAST does not specify a
pass/fail bar for Guideline 4. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, Title II of the
ADA, and the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.2, the current W3C standard,
with 86 testable success criteria across four principles) are different in kind: they
carry testable, binary conformance criteria, and 504 and Title II are enforced through
the Department of Justice, the Office for Civil Rights, and private litigation. These
are two distinct sourced figures, not one: the 2024 DOJ rule implementing Title II's
web and mobile app accessibility requirements sets WCAG 2.0 Level A and AA, not 2.2, as
its technical standard, so "the current W3C standard" and "what the 2024 rule
requires" are not the same version and shouldn't be read as interchangeable. Applying
Guideline 4 well does not by itself establish legal accessibility compliance, and a
legally compliant interface is not automatically a good 4.1 or 4.2 design: a course
"using all three UDL principles might still have inaccessible PDFs," and a
WCAG-conformant interface might still offer only one way to answer a question. See
[[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]] for the full distinction.

## In practice

**Worked pass.** A digital assignment asks learners to sequence historical events on a
timeline by dragging cards into order.

- **4.1 move:** the drag-only version has exactly one motor pathway. Add a keyboard
  path (tab to a card, press enter to pick it up, arrow to a position, enter to place
  it) and a path that works with switch-scanning software. None of this changes what's
  being assessed (sequencing events); it changes how the selection gets made
  physically.
- **4.2 move:** a learner already uses a switch device at home. Confirm the platform is
  actually compatible with it, not just "keyboard-accessible" in principle, and that the
  learner has been shown how their specific device drives this specific interface. A
  design that passes 4.1 in the abstract can still lock this learner out if 4.2 was
  skipped.
- **Physical space:** the same guideline covers the room, not only the screen. Fixed
  desks, a single counter height, and one lighting level are a single "motor pathway"
  applied to furniture. Flexible seating, adjustable-height surfaces, and controllable
  lighting are the same move applied physically: CAST's own design options for 4.1
  include "embed flexibility into the design of the physical space (e.g., flexible
  seating and positioning, lighting, etc.)."

## Gotchas & constraints

- **4.1 and 4.2 get run together.** Designing multiple input methods (4.1) does not
  guarantee any given learner has the device, software, or training to use one of them
  (4.2). They are companion considerations under one guideline, not one move.
- **UDL is not accessibility compliance.** Worth repeating at this guideline
  specifically, since the overlap is closest here: WCAG 2.2, Section 504, and ADA
  Title II are enforceable, testable legal floors; Guideline 4 is a design framework
  with none of that apparatus. See [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].
- **Do not confuse this with Guideline 5.** Guideline 4 varies the physical means of
  responding or navigating; Guideline 5 varies the medium of what gets produced. A
  learner who can operate every input device but is still forced into an essay when
  speech would show the same knowledge has a Guideline 5 problem, not a Guideline 4
  one.
- **Disclosed gap.** The staged CAST extracts for 4.1 and 4.2 reference research only
  by a link to a separate research page; no specific findings, designs, or effect
  sizes were retrieved for either consideration. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for what the broader UDL evidence literature
  does and does not support.
