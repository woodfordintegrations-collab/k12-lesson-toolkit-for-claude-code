# Audit an Existing Lesson for Barriers

## Summary

The retrofit practice: taking a lesson that already exists and systematically
examining its four components (goals, assessments, methods, materials) for
obstacles that have nothing to do with what students are supposed to learn. This is
the IRIS Center protocol, and it is what most teachers actually need, since most
lessons under review were not built from a blank page.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this when a lesson is already running, or already written, and something
about it isn't working, before assuming the problem is the students.

| The actual situation | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| You're building a new lesson from nothing | [[design-a-lesson-with-udl]] |
| The lesson is fine but the standard itself has the method baked into its wording | [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]] |
| The specific complaint is about the assessment instrument | [[design-an-accessible-assessment]] |
| You're vetting purchased curriculum materials rather than a lesson you wrote | [[evaluate-curriculum-materials-for-udl]] |

The diagnostic move throughout is the same one CAST states plainly: **"barriers live
in designs, not in students."** If a student is stuck, the audit's default assumption
is that something in the four components is stuck first.

## How it works

A barrier is anything in the lesson design that blocks access to the learning goal.
The source draws a sharp line: **construct-irrelevant barriers** are obstacles
unrelated to what you actually want students to learn; removing them is what clears
the way to the **construct-relevant** challenge: the desirable difficulty that is
genuinely part of the learning. See [[construct-irrelevant-variance]] for the
measurement version of this same distinction.

The audit runs in four steps:

1. **Clarify the learning goal.** You cannot classify a barrier as
   construct-irrelevant without a fixed target to check it against.
2. **Anticipate barriers using the UDL Guidelines as a lens** across Engagement
   ("why"), Representation ("what"), and Action & Expression ("how"), plus
   environment, materials accessibility, and pace as supporting checks.
3. **Identify specific barriers with examples**, not abstractions: the audit is only
   useful if it names the actual obstacle.
4. **Document and decide which barriers to address**, prioritizing by how many
   students are affected, how directly the barrier ties to the construct, and whether
   it can be removed universally rather than requiring individual retrofitting.

The **four-component audit (IRIS Center model)** is the concrete instrument:

| Component | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| **Goals** | Is the learning objective explicit and grade-level? Can students understand what success looks like? |
| **Assessments** | Can students demonstrate learning in multiple formats? Does the assessment measure the goal, or something else? |
| **Methods** | Are skills and content taught through multiple representations? Is pacing uniform or flexible? Is collaboration required, or is it choice? |
| **Materials** | Are resources available in multiple formats (print, digital, audio, visual)? Is vocabulary support embedded? |

## In practice

**The word-problem case.** A recurring barrier the source names directly: "after you
model how to solve a word problem, some students struggle to remember the process."
The barrier isn't math ability: it's processing overload, because the task bundles
reading text, identifying operations, executing an algorithm, and recording an answer
into one undifferentiated step. The fix runs through Methods and Materials, not
through re-teaching the math: graphic organizers highlighting the steps, short videos
showing the process, manipulatives to make steps visible, or permission to keep
written notes alongside the work.

**Worked example: elementary math (Mr. Hughes, IRIS Center).**
*Before:* students created a bar graph from a predetermined dataset on a worksheet.
*Barrier identified:* students had no agency in data choice, tool, or format. The goal
was graph comprehension, but the barriers were lack of relevance and a single
allowed format, neither of which the goal required.
*After:* students select relevant, authentic data from their own interests and choose
from multiple graphing tools and formats: paper, digital, manipulatives, digital
graphics software.

**Worked example: middle school ELA (Ms. Tong, IRIS Center).**
*Before:* all students read the same text from a single physical textbook.
*Barrier identified:* the goal was comprehension of content and themes. The barriers
were all unrelated to that goal: format (print only), modality (visual text only),
and access (single pace).
*After:* the same text offered simultaneously in multiple formats (paperback,
e-book, audiobook) so students choose modality while working toward the identical
learning goal.

Notice what stays constant across both: the *goal* (graph comprehension; text
comprehension) never moves. Only the four components around it change. That is the
audit working correctly: if the goal itself had shifted, this would be
[[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]], not a successful audit.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Not every barrier is worth fixing immediately.** The source is explicit about
  prioritizing: barriers affecting the most students, barriers most directly tied to
  the construct, and barriers removable through universal redesign rather than
  individual retrofitting. Treating the audit as a flat checklist to fully clear in
  one pass is not what the protocol asks for.
- **This audit does not replace individualized accommodations.** A well-audited Tier 1
  lesson reduces how many students need them, but does not retire an existing IEP or
  its accommodations. See [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]].
- **Audit is not the same activity as differentiation.** Differentiation reacts to an
  individual student's identified need; this audit is proactive redesign of the shared
  design, run against barriers rather than against a specific learner's profile, even
  though it is applied after the lesson already exists. See
  [[confusing-udl-with-differentiated-instruction]] for where the line actually falls.
- **A barrier scan without a fixed goal collapses.** Step 1 of the protocol,
  clarifying the goal, is not preamble; skip it and there is no way to tell a barrier
  from the construct itself.
- **This audit protocol is not itself an outcomes claim.** UDL's outcome evidence is
  moderate and how to operationalize it is contested; see
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]]. Running the audit correctly is still worth
  doing regardless of the pooled effect size.
