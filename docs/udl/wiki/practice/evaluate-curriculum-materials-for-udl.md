# Evaluate Curriculum Materials for UDL

## Summary

Four named instruments show up when practitioners talk about "evaluating curriculum
for UDL," and they are not interchangeable. This page names each precisely and says
what it actually evaluates, because using the wrong one produces a confident answer
to a question you weren't asking.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this page when you're choosing an instrument to evaluate curriculum
materials, a teacher's practice, or standards alignment, and need to know which tool
answers which question.

| The actual question | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| Is this standard's wording embedding a method into the goal? | [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]] |
| Is this specific lesson, not a whole curriculum program, free of barriers? | [[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]] |
| Is the school, not the curriculum, actually implementing UDL? | [[implement-udl-at-school-scale]] |
| Can UDL implementation be measured at all, and how reliably? | [[measuring-udl-implementation-fidelity]] |
| I'm designing a new lesson and need the sequence, not an evaluation tool | [[design-a-lesson-with-udl]] |

The diagnostic question below is not "which UDL rubric should I use": it's **"am I
evaluating the materials, the teacher's practice, or the standards alignment, because
those are three different things and only one instrument here evaluates each."**

## How it works

None of the four instruments below was built to do everything. They sit on two
different axes: what they evaluate (materials vs. practice vs. standards alignment)
and what they're for (auditing something that exists vs. building something new).
Conflating them is the single most common error in this space: treating a
practice-fidelity rubric as if it certifies a textbook, or treating a
standards-alignment tool as if it certifies UDL design.

## In practice

**EdReports 2.0 review tools (2024–2025).** EdReports maintains the most widely used
curriculum review tools in K-12. The 2.0 update covers five focus areas: **English
Language Arts** (K-12 core plus K-2 foundational skills), **Mathematics** (K-12 core
comprehensive materials), **Science** (K-12 core comprehensive materials),
**Multilingual Learner (MLL) Supports** (K-12 ELA, math, and science materials, new
in 2.0), and **Pre-Kindergarten**. Each review runs two documents: a Review Criteria
document with specific evaluation indicators, and an Evidence Guide covering
purposes, evidence collection methods, guiding questions, and scoring criteria. The
2.0 enhancements are content-specific: deepened science-of-reading and structured
literacy alignment in ELA, deepened emphasis on the Standards for Mathematical
Practice in math, phenomena-driven three-dimensional instruction in science, and the
new MLL-specific criteria evaluating language supports, accessibility, and
asset-based approaches across content areas. **What EdReports evaluates:**
instructional materials for standards alignment and usability. It is not a UDL
instrument by design: the new MLL criteria are the closest it comes to touching
accessibility and flexible language supports, and even those are one slice of the
tool, not a UDL certification.

**The CAST UDL Progression Rubric (Novak & Rodriguez).** Published through CAST
Professional Publishing, this is an 8-page downloadable rubric that measures
**educator progress in implementing the UDL Guidelines**, not a property of a
textbook or curriculum program. Its scoring levels, verbatim: Emerging, Proficient,
and "Progressing toward Expert Practice." It's used for self-evaluation by educators
and team-level progress assessment. This is the instrument most likely to be
mistaken for a materials-evaluation tool because "UDL" is in its name and its subject
matter overlaps with curriculum design decisions. It is not that. **What it
evaluates:** how well a person is applying the framework, not whether a set of
materials embodies it.

**The CAST UDL Curriculum Toolkit.** An open-source web application for building
interactive, multimedia curricula that follow UDL principles from the start, not a
rubric for scoring something that already exists. Its embedded features map onto the
three principles: comprehension supports (highlighting, accessible text, embedded
vocabulary glossaries), action/expression supports (notetaking, writing prompts,
drawing, audio recording), and engagement supports (video, teacher feedback, a
collaborative whiteboard). **What it does:** it's a construction tool. If you're
evaluating existing materials rather than building new ones, its three feature
categories are still useful as an informal checklist of what UDL-aligned material
tends to include, but using it that way is a repurposing, not its intended function.

**Achieve/Student Achievement Partners IMET.** The Instructional Materials
Evaluation Toolkit, jointly developed by Achieve and Student Achievement Partners,
evaluates the comprehensiveness of curriculum materials (ELA and math, K-12) for
alignment to the Common Core State Standards and standards-aligned pedagogy. **What
it evaluates:** CCSS alignment specifically. It predates UDL 3.0, is not a UDL
instrument, and its scope is narrower than EdReports both by subject (no science, no
MLL-specific criteria) and by focus (standards fidelity, not general usability).

**A worked pass: choosing the right instrument.** A district is deciding whether to
adopt a new middle-school math curriculum and wants to know if it's both
standards-aligned and UDL-friendly. Those are two separate questions with two
separate tools. For standards alignment, EdReports Mathematics (or IMET if the
concern is CCSS fidelity specifically) is the right instrument, and a strong
EdReports rating says nothing about UDL flexibility: it is not evaluating that.
For whether teachers are actually implementing UDL with whatever curriculum gets
adopted, the CAST UDL Progression Rubric is the right instrument, and it evaluates
practice, not the textbook. There is a real gap here, disclosed rather than papered
over: none of the four instruments in this page's sources directly certifies "this
set of curriculum materials embodies UDL's three principles." EdReports 2.0's new MLL
criteria are the closest partial answer, and the UDL Curriculum Toolkit's three
feature categories are the closest informal checklist. Neither is a substitute for
an instrument that doesn't yet exist in these sources.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **These four are not interchangeable, and the most common error is treating them
  as if they were.** EdReports and IMET evaluate materials against standards. The
  Progression Rubric evaluates a person's practice. The Curriculum Toolkit builds
  rather than evaluates. Naming which one you actually used matters when reporting
  results to a school board or a curriculum committee.
- **"UDL" in an instrument's name is not evidence it evaluates materials for UDL.**
  The CAST UDL Progression Rubric is the clearest case: its name suggests a materials
  tool and it is explicitly an educator-practice tool.
- **A strong EdReports rating is not a UDL claim.** EdReports evaluates standards
  alignment and instructional quality; only the new 2.0 MLL-specific criteria touch
  accessibility and language-support territory, and that is one focus area among
  five, not a general UDL audit.
- **IMET is CCSS-specific and pre-dates UDL 3.0 vocabulary.** Don't extend its
  findings to states that haven't adopted Common Core, or read its alignment findings
  as saying anything about flexible means.
- **Content-specific guidance is uneven.** The sources behind this page document
  deep, worked guidance for K-5 math and K-2 literacy specifically; secondary
  math, science, and ELA applications are described as emerging and less
  systematized. Treat instrument findings in those areas as thinner evidence than
  the elementary examples this page and its sources draw on.
- **None of this substitutes for a barrier audit of an actual lesson.** These
  instruments evaluate materials or practice in the aggregate. A specific lesson can
  still contain a specific barrier that no curriculum-level review catches. See
  [[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]].
