# Apply UDL to an Academic Standard

## Summary

The move that connects UDL to standards-aligned instruction: reading a standard's
wording to separate the **construct** it requires (what must be learned) from the
**means** it may have accidentally baked in (how it must be shown). Most standards
leave the means open. A minority do not, and that minority is where this practice
does its real work.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this when a standard is the starting point, before you write the learning
goal that [[design-a-lesson-with-udl]] Step 1 needs, or before you decide whether an
existing lesson's Goals component ([[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]]) is actually
construct-clean.

| The actual situation | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| The standard is settled and you're now building the flexible options around it | [[design-a-lesson-with-udl]] |
| You're auditing a lesson that already exists, not reading the standard itself | [[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]] |
| You need to build the assessment instrument, not just read the standard | [[design-an-accessible-assessment]] |
| You want the theory this practice is an application of | [[firm-goals-flexible-means]], [[construct-irrelevant-variance]] |

The diagnostic question, verbatim from Novak Education, a commercial UDL consultancy
(not CAST doctrine): **"Is this standard requiring that students all do the exact same
thing in the same way?"** If yes, the means is embedded in the goal. If no, flexibility
is already available and there is nothing further to resolve here.

## How it works

Novak Education's operationalization of the CAST principle separates standards into
two categories. This split and its verb lists are Novak's framing, not CAST's own
terminology:

- **Content standards**: demonstrate *knowledge*, and use verbs like explain,
  demonstrate, describe, analyze.
- **Method standards**: demonstrate *skill in doing something*, and use verbs like
  write, use, measure, solve.

CAST's framing of why this matters:

> "Focusing on the learning goal supports educators to identify and reduce
> construct-irrelevant barriers so learners can access and engage with the
> construct-relevant learning goals."

**Worked example.** "Write arguments" is a content goal (argumentation) with a method
(written) embedded in it. Reworded as "construct and defend a claim with evidence,"
the goal is clear and the method opens up: essay, video explanation, oral argument,
multimedia presentation are all now valid demonstrations of the same construct.

### The hard case: when the verb embeds the means

Some standards do not leave this room. The source's central worked example:

> "Print with appropriate spacing between words and sentences."

This standard has exactly one rigid expected outcome, because the means of
demonstrating it, handwriting, is the standard's entire content. A student who
understands spacing conventions perfectly but cannot handwrite will fail this
standard regardless of what they know, because there is no separable construct left
once the method is removed: the method *is* the construct here. This is the case
[[construct-irrelevant-variance]] names directly: variance in the score that comes
from something other than the thing being measured.

Other standards embed method the same way, to varying degrees:

- "Use a semicolon (and perhaps a conjunctive adverb) to link two or more closely
  related independent clauses": restricts expression to punctuation as the sole
  mode.
- "Measure and estimate liquid volumes and masses of objects using standard units":
  the method (measurement) is embedded; the underlying construct is harder to
  separate out cleanly.
- "Represent proportional relationships through equations": the representation form
  (equations) is specified as required, not optional.

### What to do with a method-embedded standard

The source gives a three-move procedure:

1. Identify the true learning construct, separately from the stated method.
2. Design flexible methods and materials that allow multiple pathways to demonstrate
   that construct.
3. Ensure the assessment captures the construct regardless of which method a student
   used.

**Reconstruction example.** Standard as written: "Write a paragraph with topic
sentence, supporting details, and conclusion." Construct identified: understanding
paragraph structure and composition. Flexible demonstration methods: written essay,
video explanation of the structure, oral presentation with a visual organizer, digital
interactive diagram.

## In practice

Run the standard through the diagnostic question first: does it require the exact
same performance in the exact same way from every student? If the verb is "explain,"
"describe," "analyze," usually no, and you can proceed straight to
[[design-a-lesson-with-udl]]. If the verb is "write," "print," "measure," "solve,"
stop and do the separation work before writing your lesson goal, because whatever you
write there will inherit the standard's constraint uncritically otherwise.

For the hard case specifically ("print with appropriate spacing"), the honest
resolution looks like this: if handwriting itself is what this unit is teaching (fine
motor formation, this grade's handwriting curriculum), the method *is* the construct
and there is no separation to make: a student genuinely must produce legible printed
spacing. If handwriting is incidental (this standard showed up inside a broader
writing unit where spacing is being used as evidence of something else, like sentence
segmentation awareness), then the construct is sentence segmentation, and it can be
demonstrated by marking word and sentence boundaries in typed text, reading spacing
aloud with pauses, or arranging word cards with correct gaps, none of which require a
pencil. **The standard's wording alone does not tell you which of these is true**:
that judgment call belongs to whoever wrote the unit this standard sits inside, and it
has to be made explicitly rather than defaulted.

One boundary the source is careful about: this practice does not eliminate standards
that are genuinely teaching a skill. A student who must learn handwriting, or a
specific writing format, or a particular communication style, still learns it: the
point is recognizing when that is actually the taught skill versus when it is an
incidental method for demonstrating something else entirely.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **The field does not have a definitive how-to for this.** The source is direct on
  this point: no authoritative, general-purpose method exists for separating
  construct from embedded means across arbitrary standards. Guidance is
  principle-based, and practical worked examples in the literature are limited to a
  handful of recurring cases: writing, printing, measurement. This page's "hard
  case" treatment above is close to the full extent of what is documented; do not
  read it as a template that generalizes cleanly to every method-embedded standard
  you encounter. **That is a real gap in the field, not a gap in this page.**
- **The separation itself can be misused to quietly lower rigor** if "the method isn't
  the construct" gets applied to a standard where the method genuinely is the
  construct. See [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]]: the failure mode
  looks identical to correct practice from the outside and is only distinguishable by
  whether the judgment about what's being taught was made honestly.
- **This is a design-time judgment, not an assessment-time one.** The source states
  the separation "happens at curriculum design time, not assessment time":
  deciding construct-vs-method after a student has already been scored invites
  exactly the motivated reasoning this practice exists to prevent.
- **A construct-clean rewording of a standard is not the same thing as legal
  accessibility.** Opening up the means a standard can be demonstrated through is a
  design decision; whether the resulting materials and formats actually work with
  assistive technology is a separate, testable question. See
  [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].
