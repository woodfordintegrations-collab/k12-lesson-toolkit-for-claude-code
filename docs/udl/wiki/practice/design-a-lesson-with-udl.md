# Design a Lesson with UDL

## Summary

The proactive design practice: build a lesson so barriers are designed out before a
student ever meets the task, rather than patched afterward. CAST prescribes an exact
order: clear goal, then barriers, then options, then assessment, then iterate, and
the order is not incidental. It is the mechanism that makes
[[firm-goals-flexible-means]] usable rather than aspirational.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this when you are building a lesson that does not exist yet, or rebuilding
one from the ground up. It is the forward-design half of implementation; the other
half is retrofit.

| The actual situation | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| The lesson already exists and something in it isn't working | [[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]] |
| You have a standard whose verb already restricts the method (e.g. "print with...") | [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]]: resolve this before Step 1, since it decides what the goal in Step 1 is allowed to say |
| The problem is specifically the assessment instrument | [[design-an-accessible-assessment]] |
| You're evaluating whether purchased curriculum already does this | [[evaluate-curriculum-materials-for-udl]] |
| You want the theory behind why goal and means must separate | [[firm-goals-flexible-means]] |

The diagnostic question is not "what options can I offer?" It is "what, precisely, is
the goal, and have I fixed that before I let myself think about options at all?"

## How it works

CAST's sequence, in the order it must run:

1. **Set a clear, specific, challenging learning goal.**
   > "The first step in applying the UDL framework to practice is to define a
   > specific, challenging learning goal. This clarity will allow you to strategically
   > mix and match guidelines and considerations that reduce barriers and support
   > every learner in reaching the learning goal."

   The goal has to be construct-relevant and stated so the challenge level is
   visible: otherwise you cannot later tell a barrier from the task itself. See
   [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]] if the goal originates in standards language.

2. **Anticipate and identify barriers.**
   > "Anticipate environmental barriers, create meaningful options, and fully embrace
   > human variability."

   The stance is explicit: "Barriers live in designs, not in students." You ask where
   students will get stuck, where they won't, and what construct-irrelevant
   variability this specific design introduces.

3. **Design flexible options** across materials, methods, environment, and
   assessment: the four components the field uses to structure this step (goals,
   assessments, methods, materials, per the IRIS Center model).

4. **Prepare assessments aligned to the same goal.**
   > "Prepare your assessment with the goals in mind. Aligning assessments to the
   > goal ensures that the assessments are measuring what you've intended."

   Both the instrument and the response modality should offer flexibility, but
   flexibility in modality only, never in what is being measured. See
   [[construct-irrelevant-variance]].

5. **Iterate.** CAST frames the whole process as cyclic, not a one-time build:
   > "Iterative describes a design process that is cyclic and includes planning,
   > testing or teaching, gathering feedback and data, and then refining."

**Why the order is the whole method.** Steps 3 and 4 only work if Step 1 is already
settled, because "is this a barrier or is this the task" is undecidable without a
fixed goal to check against. A teacher who starts by brainstorming flexible options
and writes the goal afterward will, without noticing, let the options define what
counts as the construct, which is exactly the failure
[[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]] describes. Goal first is not a
stylistic preference in the source material; it is the condition that makes the rest
of the sequence meaningful.

## In practice

**Step 1: write the goal, and write it narrow enough to check against.**
State what students must know, do, or care about, at a level of specificity that lets
you later ask "does this option change that, or just how a student reaches it?" A goal
like "understand fractions" is too broad to audit against; "compare two fractions with
different denominators and justify the comparison" is not.

**Step 2: run a barrier scan against that goal**, not against the activity as
currently imagined. Work through the three principle areas the source uses as a lens:

- *Engagement*: does the design welcome varied interests, allow effort to be
  sustained, and give learners any agency in how they engage?
- *Representation*: is content available through more than one modality, and is
  vocabulary support explicit rather than assumed?
- *Action & Expression*: can students respond and demonstrate learning in more than
  one format, and are expectations for that response clear?

Plus environment, materials accessibility, and pace: three supporting checks the
source adds alongside the three principles.

**Step 3: design options, but only where Step 2 found a barrier.** Vary materials
format, method of practice, environment, and response mode. This is where
[[optimize-choice-and-autonomy]] and the rest of Engagement's Guideline 7 supply the
menu, but the menu is scoped by what Step 2 actually found, not applied wholesale.

**Step 4: build the assessment from the same goal statement you wrote in Step 1.**
If the goal says "compare two fractions and justify the comparison," any assessment
format is valid as long as it captures comparison-and-justification; format (written,
oral, diagrammed) is open, the construct is not.

**A worked pass.** Take a goal: *students can explain the water cycle's four stages
and the causal relationship between them.* A teacher who skips to Step 3 might build a
lesson around building a physical model, because models are engaging, but a "build a
working diagram" activity quietly shifts the construct from *explaining causal
relationships* to *constructing an artifact*, and a student who can explain the
causality perfectly but struggles with fine motor assembly now fails on a dimension
the goal never asked about. Running Step 1 first catches this before it happens: the
goal is explanation of causal relationship, so Step 3's options are things like
diagramming, oral narration, a written paragraph, or an annotated sequence (anything
that can carry an explanation of causality), while a model-building requirement, if
used at all, is one *option* among those, not the assignment.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **The commonest failure mode is inversion: designing options first and checking the
  goal after.** Once options exist, they exert pressure to keep them, and the goal
  gets rewritten to match what was already built rather than the reverse. This is why
  the order is stated as a hard sequence rather than a checklist of four things to
  cover in any order.
- **"UDL means giving everyone choice" is a documented myth, not a synonym for this
  practice.** Source material is explicit that choice is one tactic, not the strategy,
  and that stopping at choice benefits no one. See
  [[choice-as-a-substitute-for-design]].
- **This practice does not lower rigor. It is frequently misread as doing so.** The
  goal stays fixed at grade-level challenge; what flexes is the pathway.
  [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]] covers the failure directly.
- **Explicit, direct instruction has a place inside this sequence.** It is not
  replaced by UDL; it becomes one component delivered through multiple means (video,
  live modeling, peer explanation) rather than the sole teaching method.
- **Iteration is not optional cleanup.** The source frames redesign after teaching
  (gathering feedback, identifying barriers that only appeared in practice) as part of
  the method, not a sign the first pass failed.
- **This is a procedure page, not an outcomes claim.** UDL's outcome evidence is
  moderate and how to operationalize it is contested; see
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]]. The sequence above remains sound practice
  either way.
