# Design Multiple Means of Representation

## Summary

Representation is the "what" of learning: how information reaches a learner and how
that learner turns it into usable knowledge. CAST's own framing is that "learners
differ in the ways they perceive and make meaning of information," so "there is not
one means of representation that will be optimal for every learner" and "providing
options for representation is essential." The principle contains three guidelines:
[[perception]] (1), [[language-and-symbols]] (2), and [[building-knowledge]] (3). This
page exists to route you to the right one rather than repeat what each already covers.

## When to reach for it

You are in the right principle when the problem is about **information getting in and
becoming knowledge**, not about what motivates a learner to engage, and not about how
a learner acts on or demonstrates what they know. Those are the other two principles:

| The actual problem | Go here instead |
|---|---|
| The learner could understand the material but isn't motivated to engage with it | [[multiple-means-of-engagement]] |
| The learner understands the material but can't navigate, respond, or show what they know | [[multiple-means-of-action-and-expression]] |

Once you're confident this is Representation, the three guidelines split the problem
by *where in the pipeline it breaks down*: not a strict sequence a lesson must pass
through, but three distinct failure points a designer can diagnose separately.

| The actual problem | Go here |
|---|---|
| The information doesn't reach the learner at all, or its display is fixed in a way that shuts some learners out | [[perception]] (Guideline 1) |
| The learner receives the information but the vocabulary, symbols, or notation carrying it is itself the barrier | [[language-and-symbols]] (Guideline 2) |
| The learner receives and decodes the information but isn't connecting, organizing, or transferring it into usable knowledge | [[building-knowledge]] (Guideline 3) |

The diagnostic question: is this a **sensing** problem, a **decoding** problem, or a
**constructing** problem? Each guideline page has its own routing table one level
down; this table only gets you to the right guideline.

## How it works

CAST's official description of the principle, verbatim:

> "Learners differ in the ways they perceive and make meaning of information." There
> is "not one means of representation that will be optimal for every learner";
> consequently, "providing options for representation is essential."

The three guidelines are also a column in CAST's 3×3 grid. Representation's three
guidelines occupy the same three rows, **Access, Support, Executive Function**, that
structure all nine guidelines across all three principles. [[perception]] is the Access
row, [[language-and-symbols]] is the Support row, and [[building-knowledge]] is the
Executive Function row. See [[access-support-executive-function]] for the full grid:
reading the nine guidelines as three unrelated lists of three, rather than a 3×3, is a
real way to miss how the framework is built.

CAST also ties this principle to the **recognition networks**, in posterior/back
regions of the brain, which it describes as networks that "sense and perceive
information, transforming it into usable knowledge." That is one leg of CAST's
three-network model: affective networks to Engagement, recognition networks to
Representation, strategic networks to Action & Expression. See
[[the-three-brain-networks]] for the current status of that claim and the dispute over
it, and [[the-neuroscience-basis-claim]] for whether the neuroscience grounding itself
holds up; this page states the mapping, not its evidentiary weight.

**The learning-styles confusion belongs here.** Representation is the principle most
often mistaken for the learning-styles hypothesis, because both talk about presenting
information in more than one way. They are not the same claim. The learning-styles
hypothesis says a learner has a preferred modality (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) and
that matching instruction to that preference improves outcomes, a claim that has not
held up under testing. What Representation actually asks for is that the **same**
information be made perceptible through more than one channel for everyone, as
redundancy and access, not as a diagnosis-and-match exercise performed on each
learner. A caption is not "the auditory learner gets audio, the visual learner gets
text." It is the same speech, available as text, to anyone who needs or prefers it at
that moment. CAST itself addresses the comparison directly rather than ignoring it, but
the defense is theoretical (proactive design for everyone vs. trait-matching for one
learner); it does not resolve the shared empirical problem that neither framework has
strong evidence of improving learning outcomes. See
[[udl-and-the-learning-styles-problem]] for the claim stated precisely and the evidence
on both sides, and [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for what the outcomes
literature does and does not support.

## In practice

There's no design-options list at the principle level; those live inside each
consideration, three or four layers down. What belongs here is the routing move
itself, worked once.

**A worked pass.** A tenth-grade history class is struggling with a unit built around
a single dense primary-source document, a 19th-century speech transcript. Where do
you start?

- If some students can't access the document at all (a screen reader can't parse a
  scanned image, or there's no non-text alternative to a wall of period prose), that's
  [[perception]]: specifically whether an alternative modality exists at all ([[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]]) before whether its display is tunable ([[customize-the-display-of-information]]).
- If students can read the document but the archaic diction, unfamiliar syntax, and
  period-specific vocabulary are what's stopping comprehension, that's
  [[language-and-symbols]]: specifically [[clarify-vocabulary-and-language-structures]].
- If students can read and understand the sentences but can't tell which parts of the
  speech are load-bearing for the unit's argument, or can't connect it to what they
  already know about the period, that's [[building-knowledge]]: specifically
  [[patterns-critical-features-and-big-ideas]] or [[connect-prior-knowledge]].

The same document can trigger all three at once for different students. The principle
doesn't collapse them into one fix; it tells you which guideline's design options
actually address which symptom.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **The three guidelines are not a required sequence.** CAST's structure implies a
  rough order (perceive, then decode, then build), but the source does not state that
  a lesson must clear Guideline 1 before Guideline 2 applies, or that these are gated
  stages. A learner can hit a Guideline 3 barrier (can't connect new material to prior
  knowledge) on content they perceive and decode just fine. Treat the three as
  distinct diagnostic lenses, not a pipeline with hard gates.
- **"Comprehension" is retired vocabulary.** Guideline 3 was named Comprehension in UDL
  2.2. If you learned the framework before 2024, look for it there: see
  [[building-knowledge]] for the full rename note. Guidelines 1 and 2 kept their names
  across the 2.2-to-3.0 revision; only Guideline 3 changed.
- **Representation is not the accessibility principle on its own.** Access barriers
  show up disproportionately in Guideline 1, but legal accessibility (WCAG 2.2,
  Section 504, ADA) is a separate, testable obligation that UDL does not by itself
  satisfy. See [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].
- **The evidence base is thinner than the framework's confidence suggests.** Everything
  above describes what CAST's design intends; it is not a claim that designing this
  way is demonstrated to improve outcomes at any particular effect size. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]].
