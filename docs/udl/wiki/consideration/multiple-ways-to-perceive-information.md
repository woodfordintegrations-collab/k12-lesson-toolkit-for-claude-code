# 1.2 Support Multiple Ways to Perceive Information

## Summary

Consideration 1.2 sits under [[perception]] in [[multiple-means-of-representation]].
CAST's claim is that "visual representations (images, graphics, animations, videos,
text) are not equally accessible to all learners" and that "non-visual alternatives
are essential for equitable access." The move is giving the *same* information a
second perceptual route, not matching content to a learner's supposed preferred
modality.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 1.2 when some learners cannot perceive information through the modality it
currently arrives in at all (not adjust it, perceive it) because of disability,
context, or the format itself being dense past usability.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| The information is perceivable but its display parameters are fixed (too small, too fast, wrong contrast) | [[customize-the-display-of-information]] (1.1) |
| You are tempted to route content by a learner's "learning style" rather than by an actual access barrier | [[udl-and-the-learning-styles-problem]]: read this first |
| The content itself lacks diverse voices or authentic portrayals | [[diversity-of-perspectives-and-identities]] (1.3) |
| The barrier is vocabulary or symbol complexity, not the channel it arrives through | [[clarify-vocabulary-and-language-structures]] (2.1) |
| The barrier is decoding text or mathematical notation itself | [[decoding-text-notation-and-symbols]] (2.2) |
| You want illustration diversity as a general design habit, not an access fix | [[illustrate-through-multiple-media]] (2.5) |

## How it works

CAST states the problem plainly: visual representations are "not equally accessible to
all learners, particularly those with visual disabilities or unfamiliarity with graphic
types," and "visual information can be quite dense, particularly with visual art."

The critical distinction (and the one this page exists to hold) is in what the design
options actually ask for. Every option in the source is a way of carrying the **same**
content through a second channel: a caption is not a different lesson for a different
kind of learner, it is the same speech rendered as text, present for everyone and
necessary for some. None of CAST's options here ask you to assess a learner's
modality preference and route them to a matching format. That is the
learning-styles hypothesis, a distinct and disputed claim that presentation matched to
a learner's preferred style improves outcomes: see
[[udl-and-the-learning-styles-problem]] for that claim on its own terms. 1.2 is a
redundancy-and-access move, made against the content, not a preference-matching move
made against the learner.

## In practice

CAST's design options, verbatim, grouped as the source groups them:

**Images, graphics, videos & animations**
- Provide descriptions (text or spoken) for all images, graphics, videos, or animations
- Use touch equivalents (tactile graphics or objects of reference) for key visuals
- Provide physical objects and spatial models to convey perspective or interaction
- Provide auditory cues for key concepts and transitions in visual information

**Text-specific strategies**
- Follow accessibility standards (NIMAS, DAISY) when creating digital text
- Allow competent aides, partners, or "interveners" to read text aloud
- Provide access to text-to-speech software

**Sound/audio alternatives**
- Use text equivalents via captions or automated speech-to-text
- Provide visual diagrams, charts, or musical notations
- Provide written transcripts for videos or auditory clips
- Provide sign languages for spoken communication
- Use visual analogues representing emphasis and prosody
- Provide visual or tactile equivalents for sound effects/alerts
- Provide visual and/or emotional description for musical interpretation

**A worked pass.** A science lesson is a narrated video with on-screen animation of a
process. Applying 1.2 does not mean building three separate videos for three
"types" of learner. It means the one video carries parallel channels: captions or a
transcript for the narration (useful to a deaf learner, and to anyone watching without
sound), audio description of the animation's key visual transitions for a blind or
low-vision learner, and (if the diagram is doing real conceptual work) a tactile
graphic or physical model of it. The lesson stays one lesson; the channels multiply.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **This is not the learning-styles hypothesis wearing new vocabulary.** The design
  options here provide redundant channels for identical content; none of them ask you
  to diagnose a learner's preferred modality and pick one format for them. Conflating
  the two is the single most common misreading of this consideration: see
  [[udl-and-the-learning-styles-problem]].
- **The named standards and tools are specific, not generic.** CAST names NIMAS
  (National Instructional Materials Accessibility Standard), DAISY (Digital Accessible
  Information System), text-to-speech software, tactile graphics, captions, and sign
  language directly. The staged source does not go further than naming them: it does
  not specify implementation requirements for NIMAS or DAISY, and this page does not
  invent any.
- **"Interveners"** is the source's own term for competent aides or partners who read
  text aloud, an unusual word choice worth preserving rather than smoothing over.
- **Providing an alternative modality is not the same claim as legal accessibility.**
  A caption track or a described-video track can itself fail a technical accessibility
  test even while satisfying this consideration's intent. See
  [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].
- **Overlaps with 1.1 but is not it.** A video with captions is a 1.2 move; being able
  to resize or recolor those captions is a 1.1 move. A single piece of content
  frequently needs both.
