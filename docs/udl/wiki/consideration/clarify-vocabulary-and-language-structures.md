# 2.1 Clarify Vocabulary, Symbols, and Language Structures

## Summary

Consideration 2.1 sits under [[language-and-symbols]] in the Representation principle.
The guideline's brief is "How might we offer, honor, and value multiple representations
of language and symbols?", and 2.1 answers the narrowest version of that question: the
*semantic* elements themselves. Words, symbols, numbers, icons, and sentence structures
are, in CAST's phrase, "differentially accessible to learners with varying backgrounds,
languages, and lexical knowledge."

## When to reach for it

Reach for 2.1 when a learner can perceive the material and can decode the symbol
system, but the specific words, symbols, or sentence structures in front of them are
unfamiliar: new vocabulary, domain jargon, an idiom, an unfamiliar equation structure,
a syntactically dense sentence.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| The learner cannot perceive the text or symbols at all (sensory) | [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] (1.2) |
| The learner cannot decode the symbol system itself (letter-sound mapping, notation fluency) | [[decoding-text-notation-and-symbols]] (2.2) |
| The barrier is the learner's home language or dialect differing from the dominant one | [[understanding-across-languages-and-dialects]] (2.3) |
| The material embeds a hierarchy about which language variety is "correct" | [[biases-in-language-and-symbols]] (2.4) |
| A concept is explained only in text and needs a non-text illustration | [[illustrate-through-multiple-media]] (2.5) |

The diagnostic question is narrow on purpose: *is the specific word, symbol, or
structure the thing standing between this learner and the content*, as opposed to the
modality it arrives in, the learner's fluency in decoding it, or the language it is
written in.

## How it works

CAST's definition:

> The semantic elements through which information is presented—words, symbols,
> numbers, icons, and language structures—are differentially accessible to learners
> with varying backgrounds, languages, and lexical knowledge. To ensure accessibility,
> key vocabulary, labels, icons, and symbols should be linked to alternate
> representations of their meaning... When sentence syntax or structural relationships
> in equations or graphics aren't obvious or familiar to learners, comprehension
> suffers.

Two barrier types are named together here and worth keeping distinct: **lexical**
barriers (a word or symbol you don't know the meaning of) and **structural** barriers
(a sentence, equation, or diagram whose relationships aren't legible even if every
individual word or symbol is familiar). The design options below address both, and a
material can fail on one axis while succeeding on the other: a text can define every
term in a glossary and still bury the reader in unparsed nested clauses.

## In practice

CAST's design options, verbatim:

- Pre-teach vocabulary and symbols, especially in ways that promote connection to
  learners' experience and prior knowledge
- Offer graphic symbols with alternative text descriptions
- Highlight how complex terms, expressions, or equations are composed of simpler words
  or symbols
- Embed support for vocabulary and symbols within the text (e.g., hyperlinks or
  footnotes to definitions, explanations, illustrations, previous coverage, or
  translations)
- Embed support for unfamiliar references within the text (e.g., domain-specific
  notation, lesser known properties and theorems, idioms, academic language,
  figurative language, mathematical language, jargon, archaic language, colloquialism,
  and dialect)
- Clarify unfamiliar syntax (in language or in math formulas) or underlying structure
  (in diagrams, graphs, illustrations, extended expositions, or narratives) through
  alternatives that highlight structural relations, make connections to previously
  learned structures, and make relationships between elements explicit

**A worked pass.** A biology passage introduces "chloroplast" and "photosynthesis"
inside a compound-complex sentence describing the light-dependent reaction. Under 2.1,
the terms get hyperlinked footnotes tying back to a simpler prior-unit definition
(pre-teach + embed support), and the sentence's causal structure gets a companion
diagram with the same clauses laid out as discrete, connected steps (clarify unfamiliar
syntax). Neither move changes what the passage says or what is being assessed
(comprehension of the process), only what stands between the learner and the words used
to describe it.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **2.1 and 2.3 overlap on translation, and the source does not draw a clean line.**
  Design option four under 2.1 lists "translations" as one way to embed support for
  vocabulary and symbols within the text; [[understanding-across-languages-and-dialects]]
  (2.3) treats making key information available in a learner's home or heritage language
  as a core strategy in its own right. Treat 2.1 as the move for a single term or phrase
  inside otherwise-familiar-language material, and 2.3 as the move when the *whole
  classroom language environment* is the design question, but the source presents these
  as overlapping menus, not a strict partition, and this page will not claim a precision
  CAST itself does not draw.
- **If the vocabulary is the construct being taught or tested, clarifying it removes
  what you're measuring.** A vocabulary quiz that links every tested word to its
  definition is not testing vocabulary anymore. This is the same
  [[firm-goals-flexible-means]] boundary that governs
  [[decoding-text-notation-and-symbols]] (2.2), applied one level up: know whether the
  word is scaffolding toward the goal or is the goal.
- **No effect sizes in this source for 2.1 specifically.** The staged extract points to
  a research subpage that was not fetched; nothing here should be read as a
  demonstrated outcome for this consideration. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for the general evidence picture.
