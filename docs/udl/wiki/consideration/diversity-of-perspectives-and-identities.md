# 1.3 Represent a Diversity of Perspectives and Identities in Authentic Ways

## Summary

Consideration 1.3 sits under [[perception]] in [[multiple-means-of-representation]].
Where 1.1 and 1.2 are about how content reaches the senses, 1.3 is about whose voices
appear in the content itself. CAST's claim: learners benefit from seeing themselves
reflected in materials, and exposure to perspectives beyond their own prevents them
from assuming their own experience is universal.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 1.3 when the barrier is not sensory access but the content itself: a
learner can see and hear the material fine, but it does not include them, or it
includes no one unlike them.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| A learner cannot perceive the content at all: a sensory barrier | [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] (1.2) |
| The material's display settings are fixed, not its content | [[customize-the-display-of-information]] (1.1) |
| The bias is in language or symbol choices rather than in whose stories are told | [[biases-in-language-and-symbols]] (2.4) |
| The bias is in which modes of expression are valued from learners, not which voices appear in materials | [[biases-in-modes-of-expression]] (5.4) |
| The concern is the learning environment feeling unsafe, not the materials themselves | [[biases-threats-and-distractions]] (7.4) |
| You are reaching for this to build motivation and belonging rather than perceptual access | [[welcoming-interests-and-identities]] (Guideline 7): 1.3 lives in Representation and is doing a related but distinct job |

## How it works

CAST's stated mechanism has two parts, and they are not the same move:

> Learners benefit from seeing themselves reflected in curriculum materials. When
> students encounter perspectives and cultures matching their own identities, it
> creates a sense of validation, affirmation, and belonging. Conversely, exclusion
> from materials can communicate that their perspectives are less valid and
> appreciated.
>
> Additionally, exposure to diverse perspectives prevents learners from assuming
> their experiences are universal. Without such exposure, students may develop
> incorrect assumptions about whose viewpoints matter.

The guideline-level overview frames these as **mirrors** (self-representation, for
validation and belonging) and **windows** (exposure to other perspectives, against
false universalizing), both functions, not one. CAST also states the harm of
omission directly: exclusion is not neutral, and it can communicate something about
whose perspective counts.

## In practice

CAST's design options, verbatim:

- Incorporate authors representing various identities (gender, race, ability,
  nationality, socioeconomic background)
- Recognize the range of people, cultures, and histories contributing to current
  understanding
- Attend to how people and cultures are portrayed in materials
- Challenge stereotypical or harmful portrayals
- Seek authentic, complex portrayals of people, cultures, histories, and world views
- Facilitate listening to diverse perspectives

Notice that the list separates two things people often collapse into one: *who is
present* ("incorporate authors representing various identities") and *how they are
portrayed* ("seek authentic, complex portrayals," "challenge stereotypical or harmful
portrayals"). A unit can satisfy the first and fail the second: a single token author
from an underrepresented identity, portrayed in a flattened or stereotyped way, is not
what this consideration asks for.

**A worked pass.** A history unit is built entirely from one textbook's chosen
authors and case studies. Applying 1.3 is not "add one more perspective." It means
auditing authorship for identity diversity, checking the portrayal patterns already
present for stereotype ("attend to how people and cultures are portrayed"), adding
primary sources from perspectives the textbook omits, and building in a structured
opportunity to hear those perspectives rather than just read a summary of them
("facilitate listening to diverse perspectives"). The audit and the addition are two
separate steps; doing only the second without the first risks adding a name without
correcting the portrayal underneath it.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Do not blur this into 1.2.** 1.2 is about modality: can the learner perceive the
  content at all. 1.3 is about content: whose perspectives the content contains. A
  fully accessible video can still fail 1.3 completely, and a 1.3-strong text can still
  fail 1.2 if it has no non-visual equivalent.
- **CAST does not give a test for "authentic."** The design options ask for "authentic,
  complex portrayals" and to "challenge stereotypical or harmful portrayals," but the
  staged source does not supply a rubric for telling authentic representation from
  tokenism. That is a real gap in the framework, not an omission on this page.
  Including a name is not the same claim as an authentic, complex portrayal: the
  source's own list treats these as separate design options for a reason.
  [[measuring-udl-implementation-fidelity]] takes up the broader problem of
  operationalizing UDL moves like this one.
- **Source scope.** This page does not extend beyond what the staged extract says.
  It does not name specific pedagogical literatures, legal anti-discrimination
  frameworks, or curriculum audit instruments: none of those appear in the source for
  this consideration, so none appear here as claims.
