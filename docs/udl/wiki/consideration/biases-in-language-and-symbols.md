# 2.4 Address Biases in the Use of Language and Symbols

## Summary

Consideration 2.4 sits under [[language-and-symbols]] in the Representation
principle. Where 2.3 is about making multiple languages and dialects *available*,
2.4 is about how language and symbol choices carry hierarchy: which variety of a
language gets treated as the default, correct standard, and which gets treated as a
deficit relative to it. CAST names the communities affected specifically: Spanish,
Arabic, AAVE (African American Vernacular English), and ASL (American Sign Language).

## When to reach for it

Reach for 2.4 when the barrier isn't lack of translation or unfamiliarity, but a
deficit framing: where a material, an example, or grading language treats a learner's
language variety as wrong rather than different.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| A dialect or language just needs to be made available for comprehension | [[understanding-across-languages-and-dialects]] (2.3) |
| The question is which perspectives, cultures, or figures appear in curriculum content | [[diversity-of-perspectives-and-identities]] (1.3) |
| The bias runs through the *mode of expression* a learner is graded on, not the language variety itself | [[biases-in-modes-of-expression]] (5.4) |
| A learner's dialect features are being penalized as if they were content errors | this page, and see the construct note below |

## How it works

CAST's diagnosis of the mechanism:

> A hierarchy of language emerges with standard English at the top, and those who do
> not speak or understand this dominant language are often perceived as having a
> deficit or disadvantage.

And the stakes of getting this wrong:

> Language is intimately interwoven with a person's culture, values, and sense of
> identity.

The guidance explicitly names disrupting messages of inferiority for speakers of
Spanish, Arabic, AAVE, and ASL. One clarification is worth making explicit here and is
**not itself part of the CAST text**: naming ASL alongside spoken and written
languages is correct because ASL is a full language with its own grammar and
structure: it is not a manual encoding of spoken or written English. Reading it as
"English on the hands" would misread exactly the kind of hierarchy this consideration
is naming.

## In practice

CAST's design options, verbatim:

- Facilitate listening and understanding of diverse languages
- Use closed captioning and sign languages when possible
- Use closed captioning in more than one language
- Label classroom items in multiple languages
- Welcome the use of multiple languages and dialects for communication, including
  speaking and writing
- Recognize that the goal of learning standard languages is not assimilation or
  monolingualism
- Avoid using ableist language (expressions that use terms associated with
  disabilities as pejoratives)
- Avoid using language and symbols associated with oppression, discrimination, or
  dehumanization

**A worked pass.** A writing rubric marks AAVE grammatical features (habitual "be,"
copula deletion) as errors on an assignment whose stated construct is argumentative
reasoning, not standard-English dialect production. This is the same
[[construct-irrelevant-variance]] problem that runs through this guideline: dialect
features are being scored as if they were the thing being measured, when the actual
learning goal is the argument's structure and evidence. Fixing the rubric to score
argumentation and leave dialect alone is a 2.4 move, not a lowering of the standard:
the construct doesn't move.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **CAST is high-level here and does not specify implementation detail**: how a
  teacher actually welcomes multiple dialects in graded writing, what a bias audit of
  existing materials looks like. [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]] and
  [[evaluate-curriculum-materials-for-udl]] are where that operational work lives; this
  page states the design principle, not the procedure.
- **2.4 and [[diversity-of-perspectives-and-identities]] (1.3) are easy to conflate.**
  Both are about whose culture and identity a classroom validates, but 1.3 is about
  which perspectives and content appear (whose history, whose examples), while 2.4 is
  specifically about the linguistic register or variety used to express things. A
  curriculum can represent diverse perspectives in its content while still penalizing
  non-standard dialects in how students are asked to write about them: that gap is
  exactly what 2.4 targets.
- **The ASL clarification above is this page's own addition, not CAST's stated text.**
  It is flagged as such rather than presented as a quotation.
- **Evidence caveat.** See [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]]; no effect sizes are
  available in this source for language-bias interventions specifically.
