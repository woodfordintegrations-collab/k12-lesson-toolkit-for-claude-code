# 2.3 Cultivate Understanding and Respect Across Languages and Dialects

## Summary

Consideration 2.3 sits under [[language-and-symbols]] in the Representation
principle. Its starting fact: "curriculum materials typically use one language,"
while "classrooms contain multilingual learners." This is where UDL meets
multilingual-learner practice directly: heritage languages, multilingual glossaries,
and translanguaging are named as core design strategies, not afterthoughts.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 2.3 when a learner's home or heritage language or dialect differs from the
dominant instructional language, and the design question is about how the classroom
treats that difference as a linguistic *environment*, not simply how to make one
piece of content readable.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| A single term or phrase just needs a definition or footnote translation | [[clarify-vocabulary-and-language-structures]] (2.1) |
| The learner's language variety is treated as a deficit rather than a difference | [[biases-in-language-and-symbols]] (2.4) |
| The question is which perspectives or identities appear in curriculum content, not language mechanics | [[diversity-of-perspectives-and-identities]] (1.3) |
| The barrier is perceptual (a learner needs audio, Braille, sign) independent of language | [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] (1.2) |

## How it works

CAST's core claim:

> Offering multiple representations, especially for key information or vocabulary, is
> an important aspect of accessibility... other languages and linguistic
> representations are valid and valued.

The guideline names a specific mechanism for why this matters: "language hierarchy
emerges in diverse environments, particularly when academic English is positioned as
the standard," and 2.3's design options are framed as deliberately countering that
hierarchy rather than merely accommodating around it.

**Translanguaging is the term to get precise, per the source's own wording.** The
design option reads: "support translanguaging, enabling learners to move fluidly
between languages and use their full linguistic capital." That is a distinct move from
translation. Translation is a discrete, one-time conversion of a fixed text from one
language into another: it is what a footnote or a glossary entry does. Translanguaging,
as the source frames it, is an ongoing pedagogical stance: it treats a multilingual
learner's entire linguistic repertoire as one integrated resource to think and
communicate with, used fluidly, rather than as separate languages kept in
compartments and switched between one at a time. Handing a student a bilingual
glossary is translation. Inviting a student to draft, reason, and discuss in whatever
mix of languages serves their thinking, then bridge to the target academic term, is
translanguaging.

## In practice

CAST's design options, verbatim:

- Offer a diverse curriculum with literary works, historical perspectives, and
  cultural practices from various linguistic backgrounds
- Promote opportunities to share cultures and backgrounds
- Celebrate linguistic diversity to showcase languages and cultures, fostering pride
  and respect among peers
- Make all key information in the dominant language also available in home or
  heritage languages
- Link key vocabulary words to definitions and pronunciations in both dominant and
  heritage languages
- Define domain-specific vocabulary using both domain-specific and common terms
- Incorporate electronic translation tools or links to multilingual glossaries on the
  web
- Embed visual, non-linguistic supports for vocabulary clarification (pictures,
  videos, etc.)
- Support translanguaging, enabling learners to move fluidly between languages and use
  their full linguistic capital
- Recognize and incorporate non-oral languages, such as sign languages

**A worked pass.** A middle school class is discussing a novel. The narrowest version
of 2.3 is a Spanish-English glossary of key terms (design option five and six above),
useful, but still translation. The fuller version is inviting students to draft
response notes in whatever language mix feels natural to their thinking, then working
with them to bridge that reasoning into the academic English term the class needs for
the shared discussion: that is translanguaging in the source's specific sense, and it
is a different design decision than handing out a glossary, not a stronger version of
the same one.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **The source does not specify translation fidelity, machine versus human
  translation, or who is responsible for producing heritage-language materials.**
  These are real implementation gaps in the framework as staged here, not omissions on
  this page.
- **Translanguaging is not "let students use a translation app."** Electronic
  translation tools are their own, separate design option in this same list. Collapsing
  translanguaging into a tooling choice loses the pedagogical stance CAST is naming:
  fluid movement across a learner's full linguistic repertoire, not a lookup.
- **UDL's inclusion of multilingual learners in its own stated scope is not the same
  claim as a civil-rights entitlement to English-learner services.** The federal
  statutory definition of UDL explicitly names students "who are limited English
  proficient" as within its intended reach, but that is a description of what UDL as
  a framework covers, not a guarantee that a UDL-designed classroom satisfies whatever
  separate civil-rights-based EL service obligations may apply. This wiki's staged
  legal source does not detail those specific obligations, so this page states the
  boundary without asserting what sits on the other side of it.
- **Evidence caveat.** See [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]]; nothing here should
  be read as a demonstrated outcome for multilingual-learner strategies specifically.
