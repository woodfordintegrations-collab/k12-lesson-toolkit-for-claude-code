# 7.4 Address Biases, Threats, and Distractions

## Summary

Consideration 7.4 sits under [[welcoming-interests-and-identities]] in the Engagement
principle. Its one-line brief from CAST is "Foster spaces to learn and take risks."
Unlike most of the framework, this consideration does not primarily ask you to
redesign a material or a task. It asks you to examine your own biases as an educator
and to build space for learners to name the threats and distractions they actually
experience: the barrier can be the room, or the person running it, not only the
worksheet.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 7.4 when the barrier is not in the material or the task design but in
whether the environment itself feels safe enough to take a risk in: a learner is
spending attention on staying safe or being unthreatened instead of on the work, and
that includes threats the educator may themselves be a source of.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| The material itself doesn't represent this learner's identity | [[diversity-of-perspectives-and-identities]] (1.3) |
| The specific language or symbols used carry bias | [[biases-in-language-and-symbols]] (2.4) |
| The required mode of expression carries bias | [[biases-in-modes-of-expression]] (5.4) |
| A policy or practice structurally excludes | [[challenge-exclusionary-practices]] (6.5) |
| The learner lacks control, not safety | [[optimize-choice-and-autonomy]] (7.1) |
| The task lacks relevance, not safety | [[relevance-value-and-authenticity]] (7.2) |

The overlap with the top four rows is real, not accidental: those are about bias or
exclusion inside a specific channel (representation, language, expression, policy).
7.4 is broader: the environment as a whole, the educator's own practice inside it, and
whether learners have a structural way to say what is happening to them. If the fix
belongs to an artifact, route to the artifact's page. If the fix belongs to the room or
the relationship, it belongs here.

## How it works

CAST's own definition:

> Educators must establish spaces where learners feel valued, supported, agentic, and
> encouraged to take risks. When learners have to focus their attention on having
> basic needs met or avoiding a negative or oppressive experience, they cannot engage
> with the learning process. Optimal environments acknowledge both overt and subtle
> threats and offer options to reduce threats and enable learning.

The mechanism is attentional: attention spent on safety is attention not available for
the task. That is why this consideration treats "overt" and "subtle" threats as
equally in scope: a subtle pattern in whose contributions get valued in a discussion
consumes the same finite attention as an overt exclusionary incident, even though only
one of those would show up in a policy review.

What makes 7.4 unusual in this framework is where CAST points the design work:

> The framework positions educators as needing to actively explore and acknowledge
> educator and learner biases and their impact. This is paired with creating space for
> learners to name the threats and distractions they experience, positioning learner
> voice as essential to creating truly welcoming environments.

Guidelines 1 through 6 mostly ask you to redesign an artifact: a text, a task, an
assessment. 7.4 asks the educator to examine their own practice as a possible source of
the barrier, and to build a structural channel for learners to name what they are
experiencing rather than assuming the educator would already know. That is a different
kind of design move than anything upstream of it in the framework, and it is the reason
7.4 is described as central to what changed in the 3.0 revision: CAST's own account of
why the revision was needed states that 3.0 "emphasizes addressing barriers rooted in
biases and systems of exclusion" and aims to "reduce barriers and more fully honor and
value every learner."

## In practice

CAST's design-options list for 7.4 is longer and more structured than most
considerations. It is preserved here in its own groupings rather than flattened into a
single list, because the groupings are the useful part.

**Create an accepting and supportive learning culture by:**

- Exploring how educator and learner biases can impact the learning environment
- Creating space and ways for learners to share the biases, threats, and distractions
  they may be experiencing
- Nurturing a culture where mistakes are a part of the learning process

**Vary the level of novelty or risk through:**

- Charts, calendars, schedules, visible timers, cues, etc., that can increase the
  predictability of daily activities and transitions
- Creation of class routines
- Alerts and previews that can help learners anticipate and prepare for changes in
  activities, schedules, and novel events
- Options that can, in contrast to the above, maximize the unexpected, surprising, or
  novel in highly routinized activities

**Vary the level of sensory stimulation through:**

- Variation in the presence of background noise or visual stimulation, noise buffers,
  number of features or items presented at a time
- Variation in pace of work, length of work sessions, availability of breaks or
  time-outs, or timing or sequence of activities

**Vary the social demands** required for learning or performance, the perceived level
of support and protection, and the requirements for public display and evaluation.

**Acknowledge negative experiences** within learning environments and take steps such
as shared agreements or self-regulation options to make learners comfortable.

**A worked pass.** A class discussion runs on cold-calling, and one learner has gone
nearly silent. The material is accessible and the questions are relevant, so the fix
is not upstream. Applying 7.4: vary the social demand (allow a written response before
a spoken one, make participation opt-in rather than cold-called), increase
predictability (preview the discussion questions and the norms for how mistakes are
handled before the session), and name the option explicitly rather than assuming the
learner will ask for it. This does not replace an individualized accommodation the
learner may already have (see [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]]); it is a
design change to the room that some learners need and none are harmed by.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **This consideration names the educator, not just the material, as a possible source
  of the barrier.** That is unusual in this framework and worth stating plainly rather
  than softening: most of Guidelines 1 through 6 do not ask this.
- **Overt and subtle threats are both in scope.** Do not read this narrowly as only
  about extreme incidents; CAST's text names both explicitly.
- **This is not the same work as fixing biased representation, language, expression,
  or policy.** Those have their own pages (1.3, 2.4, 5.4, 6.5) and are about specific
  channels. 7.4 is about the environment and the educator's own practice, plus a
  structural channel for learner voice.
- **This is a design goal, not an enforceable standard.** Psychological safety and a
  welcoming environment are not tested the way Section 504, ADA, or WCAG 2.2 are. See
  [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].
- **Evidence caveat.** Engagement effects for UDL as a whole rest on a literature with
  weak designs and no consensus operationalization; nothing on this page should be read
  as a demonstrated effect size for this consideration specifically. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]].
