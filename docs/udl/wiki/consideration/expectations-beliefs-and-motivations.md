# 9.1 Recognize Expectations, Beliefs, and Motivations

## Summary

Consideration 9.1 sits under [[emotional-capacity]] (Guideline 9) in the Engagement
principle, [[multiple-means-of-engagement]]. It concerns the personal knowledge a
learner has about what motivates them, their capacity to set goals they believe are
reachable, and the support they need to stay with a goal through frustration and
anxiety on the way there.

Guideline 9 was called Self-Regulation in UDL 2.2. In 3.0 it is Emotional Capacity,
and CAST's own guiding question for the whole guideline is "How might we design
learning environments that support and extend emotional capacity while honoring
variability among learners?" That is a question about the environment, not a diagnosis of
the learner. Every consideration under Guideline 9, including this one, inherits that
framing: the design options below are things you build into a classroom, not things
you ask a learner to fix in themselves.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 9.1 when a learner's own beliefs about themselves (whether this goal is
even reachable, what actually motivates them, whether frustration mid-task means
"stop" or "keep going") look like what is actually costing engagement.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| They don't yet have the skill of structuring a goal (breaking it into reachable steps) | [[set-meaningful-goals]] (6.1) |
| They don't see why the goal matters to them | [[meaning-and-purpose-of-goals]] (8.1) |
| The block is bias in how the *environment* treats them broadly, not their own self-belief | [[biases-threats-and-distractions]] (7.4) |
| They need an in-the-moment coping strategy for an emotional response, not a goal-setting practice | [[awareness-of-self-and-others]] (9.2) |
| The need is a structured look-back on what happened, not a forward-looking goal | [[individual-and-collective-reflection]] (9.3) |

The diagnostic question is whether the learner's belief about whether *they* can
succeed is the actual bottleneck, as distinct from whether they have the skill, the
reason, or the moment-to-moment coping tools.

## How it works

CAST's own definition:

> One important aspect of growing emotional capacity is the personal knowledge each
> learner has about what they find motivating, be it intrinsic or extrinsic. To
> accomplish this, learners can set personal goals that can be realistically reached,
> as well as foster positive beliefs that their goals can be met. However, learners
> also need to be supported to deal with frustration and anxiety when they are in the
> process of meeting their goals. Multiple options can be embedded into the learning
> environment to support learners to find inspiration in the learning process and to
> develop confidence in themselves as learners.

Two things travel together here: **realistic goal-setting** and **belief that the
goal can be met**. CAST treats them as a pair, not a sequence: a goal that is
technically reachable still fails this consideration if the learner does not believe
it is reachable, and support for frustration and anxiety is named explicitly as part
of the same design problem, not a separate one.

**A note on what CAST does not say here.** The staged source for this guideline
confirms that CAST's Guideline 9 pages do not explicitly name SEL (social-emotional
learning) or CASEL (the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning)
anywhere in the fetched text: not on the guideline overview, not on this
consideration. This is worth flagging because a reader who already knows SEL will
recognize a lot of what's here (goal-setting, coping with frustration, self-belief)
and may assume Guideline 9 is simply SEL relabeled. Based on what was fetched, CAST
does not make that connection explicit; it uses its own UDL-specific vocabulary
throughout. This is an observation about the pages retrieved for this wiki, not a
claim about CAST's entire published corpus.

## In practice

CAST's design options for 9.1, verbatim:

**Use prompts, reminders, guides, rubrics, and checklists that focus on:**
- Setting regulatory goals like managing anxiety in response to challenge
- Increasing the length of on-task orientation in the face of distractions
- Elevating the frequency of self-reflection

**Provide coaches, mentors, or agents that model the process of setting personally
appropriate goals that take into account both strengths and weaknesses.**

**Support activities that encourage self-reflection and appreciation of one's
strengths in order to build confidence.**

**Examine the explicit and implicit expectations set by the environment and consider
how bias may create barriers, such as low expectations or expecting every learner to
demonstrate engagement in the same way.**

Notice that the fourth option turns the lens outward. It is not a strategy for the
learner at all. It is an audit of the environment's own expectations. A teacher who
praises fast finishers, or grades "engagement" by how often a hand goes up, is
setting an implicit standard that some learners cannot meet regardless of their
actual motivation, and 9.1 asks you to check for that before assuming the gap is
inside the learner.

**A worked pass.** A learner avoids challenging problems and says "I'm just not a
math person." Applying 9.1 does not mean a pep talk. It means pairing a genuinely
reachable next-step goal (not "master fractions," but "get through today's three
problems, using the strategy sheet when stuck") with a prompt that reframes
frustration as information rather than verdict, and separately checking whether the
classroom's own signals (who gets called "naturally good at this," what pace counts
as normal) are themselves manufacturing the belief that started the whole pattern.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **This is not a therapy protocol.** "Coaches, mentors, or agents" and
  self-reflection prompts are classroom-level design moves. A learner whose anxiety
  or frustration is severe or persistent needs a referral to counseling or clinical
  support, a different system than lesson design, and UDL does not claim to be that
  system.
- **CAST does not quantify how much support is enough**, or how a designer would know
  a learner's confidence has reached a workable level. The guidance is directional,
  not measurable, and that gap is a real feature of the framework rather than
  something missing from this page.
- **The bias-audit option can drift into a generic equity exercise if it stops at
  "examine expectations" and never changes a specific practice.** CAST names concrete
  failure patterns (low expectations, uniform engagement standards) and the design
  move is to find and change those, not to hold a general conversation about bias.
- **Evidence caveat.** Engagement outcomes for UDL as a whole rest on a literature
  CAST itself does not claim is settled; nothing here should be read as a demonstrated
  effect size for this consideration specifically. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]].
