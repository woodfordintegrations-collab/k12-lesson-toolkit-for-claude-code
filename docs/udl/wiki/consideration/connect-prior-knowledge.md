# 3.1 Connect Prior Knowledge to New Learning

## Summary

Consideration 3.1 sits under [[building-knowledge]] in [[multiple-means-of-representation]].
CAST's own page title for the guideline is "Design Options for Building Knowledge":
the 3.0 rename from "provide" to "design" language applies here too. Guideline 3's
guiding question is "How might we incorporate multiple ways for building individual and
collective knowledge?", and 3.1 is the first move in answering it: new material only
lands where a foundation already exists to receive it. The design move is to build
connections to learners' prior understandings and experiences.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 3.1 when learners are stalling on new material specifically because it
assumes a foundation some of them don't yet have, not because the new content is
poorly explained, undecodable, unmotivating, or asking them to do something they
can't yet do.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| They can't decode the words or symbols carrying the new content | [[decoding-text-notation-and-symbols]] (2.2) |
| The vocabulary is unfamiliar, not the underlying idea | [[clarify-vocabulary-and-language-structures]] (2.1) |
| They have the prerequisite but can't tell what's important once the new material starts | [[patterns-critical-features-and-big-ideas]] (3.2) |
| They learned it here but can't use it somewhere else | [[transfer-and-generalization]] (3.4) |
| The way knowledge is being built here privileges one epistemology over another | [[multiple-ways-of-knowing]] (3.3) |

The diagnostic question is "is a missing or unrecognized foundation the reason this
isn't landing?" It is not "could I add a warm-up activity here?" A KWL chart bolted
onto a lesson that was actually failing for a different reason wastes the slot.

## How it works

CAST frames the barrier in two directions, and both are the actual target of this
consideration (per the staged extract, paraphrasing CAST's page rather than quoting
it): learners understand new information more readily when instruction activates or
supplies prerequisite knowledge. Barriers arise when background knowledge unfamiliar
to some learners is required to integrate the new material, but also, separately,
when learners already possess the relevant background and don't recognize that it
applies here. Reducing the barrier means both supplying what's missing and surfacing
what's dormant; an activity that only does the second (e.g. "brainstorm what you
already know") leaves the first group with nothing to anchor to.

The staged source's own annotation (not CAST's wording) maps this move onto
**schema theory**: prior knowledge functions as a structure new information attaches
to, and instruction that fails to activate it forces learners to build understanding
with no scaffold to attach it to. It also names **memory**, through prerequisite
knowledge activation and bridging strategies. CAST's own text does not use either
term; this labeling is the staging layer's translation, and it is precise enough to
use, but it isn't a CAST citation.

## In practice

CAST's design options for 3.1:

- Anchor instruction by linking to and activating relevant prior knowledge (visual
  imagery, concept anchoring, or concept mastery routines)
- Use advance organizers (KWL methods, concept maps)
- Pre-teach critical prerequisite concepts through demonstration or models
- Bridge concepts with relevant analogies and metaphors
- Make explicit cross-curricular connections (e.g., teaching literacy strategies in
  the social studies classroom)

**A worked pass.** A unit opens with photosynthesis. Some learners have never
encountered "stoma," "chlorophyll," or even a labeled diagram of a leaf's internal
structure; others have the vocabulary from a prior unit but don't yet know it's about
to matter. A KWL chart surfaces the second group's knowledge and makes it visible and
usable, but it does nothing for the first group, who have nothing to write in the
"K" column. Pre-teaching the prerequisite labels with a diagram, run before the KWL
discussion, actually supplies what's missing rather than just soliciting it. Both
moves are 3.1; neither alone covers the barrier. See [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]] for
reading a standard to identify what counts as prerequisite versus what is the
construct itself.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Activating prior knowledge is not assessing it for a grade.** A KWL chart or
  advance organizer is formative. Turn it into a scored pre-test and you've created a
  construct-irrelevant barrier of your own: see [[firm-goals-flexible-means]].
- **A single activity usually serves only one direction of the barrier.** Discussion-
  and-brainstorm formats surface existing-but-dormant knowledge; they don't supply
  knowledge that's genuinely absent. Confusing the two means half the class gets
  nothing from the activation step.
- **CAST does not specify how much anchoring is enough** or when pre-teaching tips
  into re-teaching the whole unit in miniature. This is a real gap in the framework,
  the same shape as the gap [[measuring-udl-implementation-fidelity]] takes up for
  implementation generally.
- **Evidence caveat.** Prior-knowledge activation is well established in general
  cognitive science, but the staged source gives no CAST-specific effect size for
  this consideration. Nothing here should be read as a demonstrated effect for 3.1
  specifically: see [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]].
