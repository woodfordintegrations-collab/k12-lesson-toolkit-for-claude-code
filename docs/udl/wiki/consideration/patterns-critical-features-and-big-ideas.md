# 3.2 Highlight and Explore Patterns, Critical Features, Big Ideas, and Relationships

## Summary

Consideration 3.2 sits under [[building-knowledge]] in [[multiple-means-of-representation]].
Its one-line brief is "Accentuate important information and how it relates to the
learning goal." Where 3.1 supplies the foundation, 3.2 addresses a different failure:
learners who can access the material and even understand individual pieces of it, but
can't tell which pieces are the load-bearing ones.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 3.2 when a learner's attention (and study time) is spread evenly across
material that isn't actually weighted evenly: everything looks equally important
because nothing is signaling otherwise.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| They can't perceive the information through their available channel at all | [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] (1.2) |
| The display itself can't be adjusted to their needs (font, contrast, pace) | [[customize-the-display-of-information]] (1.1) |
| The concept exists in only one medium and that medium doesn't work for them | [[illustrate-through-multiple-media]] (2.5) |
| They have no foundation to attach the highlighted feature to in the first place | [[connect-prior-knowledge]] (3.1) |
| They can now identify what matters here but can't use it in a new problem | [[transfer-and-generalization]] (3.4) |

3.2 is easy to confuse with 2.5, and the two are answering different questions. 2.5 is
about **which channel** carries a concept: text, diagram, audio, simulation. 3.2 is
about **which features, within whatever channel a learner is using, matter most**.
Illustrating a concept three different ways (2.5) doesn't tell a learner which parts
of any of those three illustrations are the ones worth attending to (3.2). They're
independent design moves and a page can need one without the other.

## How it works

CAST states the underlying claim directly, and it is worth quoting exactly because
the claim is an assertion made in the Guidelines rather than a citation to a specific
study:

> "One of the big differences between experts and novices in any domain is the
> facility with which they distinguish what is critical from what is unimportant or
> irrelevant."

No author, source, or effect size accompanies this line in either staged extract. It
functions as the framework's own justification for the consideration, not as a
reported research finding: treat it as doctrine to apply on its logic, not as an
evidenced instructional effect.

CAST's stated mechanism for closing that gap:

> "One of the most effective ways to make information more accessible is to use
> explicit cues or prompts that assist learners in attending to those features that
> matter most."

The staged source's own annotation (again, its wording, not CAST's) maps this
consideration onto **schema theory** (integrating the highlighted information into
existing knowledge) and **transfer** (previously learned skills applied to unfamiliar
problems). CAST does not name a specific cueing or signaling literature to justify why
explicit cues work; the framework asserts the design move without citing the
mechanism, and that gap is worth reporting rather than papering over with an imported
citation.

## In practice

CAST's design options for 3.2:

- Highlight or emphasize key elements in text, graphics, diagrams, or formulas
- Use outlines, graphic organizers, unit organizer routines, concept organizer
  routines, and concept mastery routines to emphasize key ideas and relationships
- Use multiple examples and non-examples to emphasize critical features
- Use cues and prompts to draw attention to critical features
- Highlight previously learned skills that can be used to solve unfamiliar problems

**A worked pass.** A dense primary-source document in a history class reads as
uniformly weighted to a novice: every sentence looks like it might matter. If the
learning goal is evaluating an argument's evidence, the highlighting should mark
evidentiary claims specifically, not unfamiliar vocabulary (that's 2.1/2.2 territory)
and not every clause. A contrasting pair, one paragraph that makes a well-supported
claim and one that doesn't (a non-example), does more to build the discrimination skill
than annotating a single "correct" passage, because it forces the comparison CAST's
expert/novice claim is actually describing.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Cueing that narrows attention can also do the learner's thinking for them.**
  Highlighting the critical features so heavily that identifying them is no longer
  the learner's job pre-digests the exact analytical work the task is supposed to
  require: see [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]] for the general failure
  mode of quietly shifting the construct.
- **The design options teach the skill; they aren't meant to substitute for it
  permanently.** Fading the cueing over time as learners build the discrimination
  skill is a different design move, handled on its own page:
  [[build-fluencies-with-graduated-support]] (5.3).
- **CAST's expert/novice claim is an assertion in the Guidelines, not a citation.** No
  study, sample, or effect size travels with it in the staged source. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for the evidence-honesty layer applied to UDL
  as a whole.
