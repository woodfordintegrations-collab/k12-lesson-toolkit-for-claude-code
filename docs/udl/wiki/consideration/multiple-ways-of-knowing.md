# 3.3 Cultivate Multiple Ways of Knowing and Making Meaning

## Summary

Consideration 3.3 sits under [[building-knowledge]] in [[multiple-means-of-representation]].
Its brief is to honor multiple and diverse ways of developing understandings of the
world. This is the **epistemological** consideration in Guideline 3: it concerns
whose process of constructing knowledge counts as legitimate, not which sensory
channel or media format carries the content. That distinction is the entire point of
this page, because 3.3 gets collapsed into its modal neighbors constantly.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 3.3 when the barrier is that the curriculum recognizes only one legitimate
process for building understanding (typically individual, linear, written analysis)
and a learner whose way of constructing knowledge looks different (storytelling,
relational or interpersonal reasoning, holistic pattern-based thinking) is being read
as "not getting it," when what's actually happening is a mismatch in epistemology, not
a gap in ability or access.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| They can't perceive the content through their sensory channel | [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] (1.2) |
| The content is presented in only one media format | [[illustrate-through-multiple-media]] (2.5) |
| The content depicts only some identities or perspectives, not others | [[diversity-of-perspectives-and-identities]] (1.3) |
| A learner's *process* of building understanding (not their access to it) is treated as invalid or absent | 3.3, this page |

**Why the confusion happens:** 1.2 and 2.5 are both about modality: how information
reaches the learner or is presented to them. 3.3 is about method: how the learner is
permitted to build understanding once information has reached them, and whether that
method is recognized as producing real knowledge at all. A learner can have full
sensory access and three different media formats in front of them (1.2 and 2.5 fully
satisfied) and still hit a 3.3 barrier if the only accepted evidence of understanding
is a single individual written analysis, and their route to that understanding was
collective, oral, or relational. It's also easy to fold 3.3 into differentiated
instruction, or into the learning-styles hypothesis. But 3.3 is not a claim that each
learner has a fixed cognitive style that instruction should be matched to. It's an
equity claim about which knowledge-building traditions a given design already
privileges. See [[udl-and-the-learning-styles-problem]] for the evidence-side critique
of the nearby claim this page is easy to mistake itself for.

## How it works

CAST states the core claim directly:

> "Equitable learning environments guide learners in the process of constructing
> knowledge by valuing and cultivating multiple ways of knowing and making meaning."

The staged extract goes on to describe (in its own paraphrase, not a CAST quotation)
that diverse cultural perspectives (including Indigenous knowledge systems that
emphasize holistic, interconnected understanding) can enrich methodologies that
default to a single, typically Western, analytical mode. Well-designed materials
incorporate multiple knowledge-building approaches: problem solving, storytelling,
algorithms, holistic and linear thinking, combined with models, scaffolds, and
feedback that help learners apply different strategies effectively.

Worth naming precisely: CAST bundles two different kinds of design moves under this
one consideration. One is epistemological: storytelling, relational learning,
multiple entry points, choice of organizational method. The other is cognitive-load
management: chunking information, progressively releasing it, removing unnecessary
distractions. The staged source's own annotation labels the second group with
**cognitive load theory** and **schema building through multiple entry points**. These
are legitimate design moves, but they are not the same move as recognizing a
non-dominant way of knowing as valid. A designer applying 3.3 should know which kind
of barrier they're actually solving before reaching for a design option: chunking a
text does nothing for a learner whose way of knowing is being excluded, and honoring
storytelling as a valid method does nothing about cognitive overload.

## In practice

CAST's design options for 3.3, grouped by the distinction above:

*Epistemological:*
- Incorporate multiple ways of knowing, including storytelling, kinesthetics, problem
  solving, and relational learning through interpersonal experiences
- Use options for organizational methods and approaches (e.g., tables and algorithms
  for mathematical operations)
- Provide multiple entry points to lessons and optional pathways through content
- Use interactive models that guide exploration and new understandings

*Cognitive-load management:*
- Use explicit prompts for each step in sequential processes
- Introduce graduated scaffolds that support information processing strategies
- "Chunk" information into smaller elements, preventing cognitive overload
- Progressively release information (e.g., sequential highlighting)
- Remove unnecessary distractions unless essential to instructional goals

**A worked pass.** A unit that accepts only a single written analytical essay as
evidence of "understanding a historical event" is enforcing one epistemology. Opening
the assessment to an oral history interview format, a chronological or relational
retelling, or a collectively authored explanation are all still evidence of the same
construct (the event, its causes, its significance) built and expressed through a
different way of knowing. The construct stays fixed; the epistemological route to it
varies. See [[firm-goals-flexible-means]] for why that distinction is what keeps this
from becoming a lowering of rigor.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **The single biggest misreading is folding 3.3 into 1.2 or 2.5** because all three
  involve "multiple ___." They answer different questions: perceptual channel,
  illustrative format, and legitimacy of a knowledge-building method. If a page's
  barrier can be fixed by a different modality or format alone, it wasn't a 3.3
  problem.
- **3.3 is an equity and legitimacy claim, not an empirical claim about matching
  instruction to fixed individual traits.** Reading it as "everyone has their own way
  of knowing, so match instruction to it" collapses it into the learning-styles
  hypothesis, which the evidence base does not support as an effective-instruction
  claim: see [[udl-and-the-learning-styles-problem]]. CAST's claim here is about
  which processes are recognized as legitimate, independent of whether matching
  instruction to a learner's preferred style improves outcomes.
- **CAST bundles epistemological and cognitive-load design options under one
  consideration.** They solve different barriers. Chunking a dense text does not
  address a learner whose way of knowing is being excluded from the assessment.
- **No number or effect size accompanies this consideration in the staged source.**
  See [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for the evidence layer generally.
