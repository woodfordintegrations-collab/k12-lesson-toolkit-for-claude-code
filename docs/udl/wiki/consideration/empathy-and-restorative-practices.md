# 9.4 Cultivate Empathy and Restorative Practices

## Summary

Consideration 9.4 sits under [[emotional-capacity]] (Guideline 9) in the Engagement
principle, [[multiple-means-of-engagement]]. It carries two distinct definitions from
CAST: empathy, the perspective-taking that builds and sustains a caring community, and
restorative practices, a specific accountability-and-repair response to broken trust
that CAST explicitly contrasts with punishment.

Restorative practice is a defined tradition in school discipline, not a general
synonym for kindness. This page treats it that precisely, because reading it loosely
is the most likely misuse of this consideration.

## When to reach for it

Reach for 9.4 specifically when trust in the learning community has been damaged by
an action's impact (intentional or not) and needs repair, or when you are
proactively building the cross-difference perspective-taking that makes such ruptures
less frequent.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| No specific rupture yet: you're building general self/other awareness | [[awareness-of-self-and-others]] (9.2) |
| The need is a structured look-back on learning, not repair after harm | [[individual-and-collective-reflection]] (9.3) |
| The felt need is belonging broadly, not repair after a specific breach | [[belonging-and-community]] (8.4) |
| Content itself lacks authentic representation of diverse experience | [[diversity-of-perspectives-and-identities]] (1.3) |
| The situation involves safety, legal exposure, or a level of harm that triggers formal discipline policy | outside UDL's scope: this is a school discipline or administrative process, not a design fix |

## How it works

CAST gives two separate definitions here. On empathy:

> Empathy is an essential component for co-designing equitable learning environments.
> Human beings naturally feel a sense of empathy toward others with whom they identify
> or have a common experience. Additional care and awareness are required to cultivate
> a sense of empathy with those who may appear different or come from different
> backgrounds. Activities that invite listening to and considering perspectives other
> than one's own, building communication skills, and offering content that
> authentically represents a diversity of lived experiences can help expand learners'
> empathy for one another. Empathy has the potential to build and sustain a caring
> learning community. This attention to others' experiences and perspectives
> strengthens the communal bonds within a learning environment, creating a space in
> which learners feel safe taking risks and collaborative learning can take place.

On restorative practices, CAST's own definition, quoted directly, not paraphrased:

> Sometimes, trust is broken when care is not extended toward others. This may not be
> intentional, as sometimes we are not aware of how our actions impact others. While
> it can be challenging for some to confront these situations, restorative practices
> are a powerful vehicle for repair. **Rather than punishing learners for mistakes,
> restorative practices allow for learners to consider the impact of their actions and
> design an appropriate response to repair trust in the community.** Empathy is a key
> component to restorative practices, as learners practice identifying with the
> perspective of others, understand the difference between intent and impact, and
> seek to make the learning community whole.

The bound is explicit in the text: restorative practices are defined **against**
punishment, not as a synonym for it or a softer version of it. The mechanism is
accountability through impact-awareness and repair: the learner names what happened,
separates intent from impact, and designs the response. It is not the absence of any
consequence.

**A note on what CAST does not say here.** The staged source for this page confirms
that CAST's text for 9.4 does not explicitly reference SEL, CASEL, or
trauma-informed-practice terminology, even though empathy, perspective-taking, and
circle protocols closely parallel both restorative-justice and trauma-informed
traditions outside UDL. Based on what was fetched, CAST keeps its own vocabulary here
rather than cross-referencing those fields by name. This is an observation about the
pages retrieved for this wiki, not a claim about CAST's wider published output.

## In practice

CAST's design options for 9.4, verbatim:

- Use a strategy such as circle practice (a protocol where learners share about their
  emotions and experiences by passing a talking stick and responding one by one) or
  an emotions check-in to encourage learners to learn from one another's perspectives.
- Facilitate learners sharing coping strategies or coping needs to encourage learners
  to take care of one another.
- Institute protocols such as a "buddy bench" or an accountability partner to
  encourage communal responsibility for the learning community.
- Co-create and facilitate classroom agreements. Specifically ask learners to add to
  the agreements the things they need to feel safe in the classroom.
- Create kind and equitable tools, processes, and protocols for learners to hold one
  another accountable to community agreements in classrooms, workplace settings, and
  other learning environments.
- Use a protocol such as circle practice to make communal decisions about how to
  restore the community after an agreement has been broken. These protocols can span
  primary through adult learner settings.

**A worked pass.** In a group project, one learner's contribution is repeatedly
dismissed by teammates, and trust in the group breaks down. Applying 9.4 does not
mean a grade penalty for the dismissive learners, since that is a discipline-policy
lever, outside this consideration. It means a structured conversation, following the
circle-practice pattern above: the affected learner names the impact, the group
works through intent versus impact, and together they design a specific, concrete
change to how they will work together, consistent with the community-agreement
protocol CAST names, and aimed at repair rather than sanction.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Restorative practice is a specific tradition, not a loose synonym for
  empathy or kindness.** CAST's own definition is precise: accountability, impact
  over intent, a designed response to repair trust, and a page or lesson that just
  means "be nice to each other" is not applying this consideration as CAST wrote it.
- **This complements formal discipline policy; it does not replace it.** CAST's design
  options here are classroom protocols (circle practice, buddy benches, community
  agreements), not a legal or administrative process. Where an incident involves
  safety concerns or triggers a school's formal disciplinary or legal obligations,
  that process governs, and 9.4 is not a substitute for it.
- **This is a design framework, not a clinical or therapeutic intervention.** Empathy-
  building and repair protocols are structured classroom activities. A rupture that
  reflects deeper relational or mental-health needs calls for counseling or
  specialized support, a different system than UDL.
- **Evidence caveat.** Nothing on this page should be read as a demonstrated effect
  size for restorative practices or empathy-building specifically; see
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for what the broader UDL literature does and
  does not support.
