# Proactive Design vs Retrofit

## Summary

Proactive design means building flexibility into goals, methods, materials, and
assessments at design time, anticipating variability before a lesson is ever taught.
Retrofit means adapting a curriculum that already exists, after the fact. CAST prefers
proactive design and says so explicitly. It does not say retrofitting is illegitimate,
and it does not say proactive design retires the need for individual accommodations.

The diagnostic question is not which approach is more correct. It's where in the
curriculum's lifecycle you're actually standing, and what that changes about what you
should do next. Most educators are standing in the retrofit position: working with
curriculum they didn't write, on a timeline that doesn't include a redesign. That is
not a compromised version of UDL. It's the version most people actually practice.

## How it works

CAST's own statement of the preference: "Universal Design for Learning refers to a
process by which a curriculum (including goals, methods, materials, and assessments)
is intentionally and systematically designed from the beginning to address individual
differences. With curricula designed with UDL principles, the difficulties and
expenses of subsequent 'retrofitting' and adaptation of 'disabled' curricula can be
reduced or eliminated." The proactive stance follows from this: "we can and should
proactively anticipate and plan for differences in learner variability," which means
setting clear, rigorous goals, anticipating barriers, and designing to minimize those
barriers before teaching begins.

The following caveat is load-bearing for this page, and the staged source frames it
as its own analytical caveat rather than a CAST quotation: while CAST clearly prefers
proactive design, retrofitting existing curricula using UDL principles is possible and
does occur in practice. The preference is not a prohibition; it is a matter of
efficiency and effectiveness.

The same design sequence runs either way: set the goal, anticipate barriers, design
flexible options, align the assessment. Proactive design runs it forward, before a
single lesson happens. Retrofit runs essentially the same audit backward, against
material that already has a fixed shape, and often against a goal that was never
explicitly stated and has to be reconstructed before the audit can even start.

## In practice

The retrofit path, read as a sequence:

1. **Clarify the learning goal.** In an inherited lesson this is frequently implicit.
   Reconstruct it before doing anything else, since every later judgment about which
   barriers are construct-irrelevant depends on knowing what the construct actually is.
2. **Anticipate barriers** across engagement, representation, and action/expression,
   auditing each of the four components in turn: are the **goals** explicit and
   grade-level; can students demonstrate learning in multiple formats on the
   **assessment**; are content and skills taught through multiple **methods** and
   flexible pacing; are **materials** available in more than one format.
3. **Identify specific barriers with examples.** A word-problem case from the
   implementation literature: after modeling how to solve a word problem, some
   students struggle to remember the process, not because they lack the math but
   because they're handling several cognitive demands at once (read text, identify
   the operation, execute it, record the answer). The fix (graphic organizers, a short
   video of the process, manipulatives, written notes) removes the processing barrier
   without touching the math.
4. **Document and prioritize.** Not every barrier gets fixed at once. Prioritize
   barriers affecting the most students, barriers most directly tied to the construct,
   and barriers that are removable through a universal redesign rather than
   case-by-case, individual retrofitting.

Two worked examples from the implementation literature are retrofits in this exact
sense, even though the source material doesn't label them that way. **Before:**
students built a bar graph from a single predetermined dataset on a worksheet. The
barrier was no agency in data, tool, or format, not a math deficit. **After:** students
choose relevant data from their own interests and pick from multiple graphing tools and
formats. Separately: **before**, all students read the same text from one physical
textbook; the barriers were format (print only), modality (visual only), and pace
(single speed), not comprehension. **After:** the same text is offered simultaneously
as paperback, e-book, and audiobook, so students choose modality while working toward
the same comprehension goal. Both start from an existing lesson. Both are UDL.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Sources here do not establish that proactive design eliminates the need for
  individual accommodations, and the disability-studies critique disputes it
  directly.** From that literature: "UDL's promise to eliminate the need for
  individualized accommodations through universal design overstates what design alone
  can achieve. Disabled students and disability scholars argue that disclosure,
  accommodations, and individualized support remain necessary even in well-designed
  UDL courses." And, more concretely: "The research suggests that UDL cannot entirely
  replace individualized support. Accommodations must be selected based on individual
  student needs and there is no 'one size fits all' solution." Individualized support
  remains necessary even where universal design is done well. See
  [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]].
- **Retrofit is real work, not lesser work, but it inherits a real ceiling.** You can
  only flex what the existing materials, assessment format, and institutional
  constraints will let you touch. Proactive design has no such ceiling, because the
  constraint hasn't been built into anything yet. That is the actual argument for
  preferring it, not that retrofit produces a worse outcome.
- **The strongest illustrations of "doing UDL" in the implementation literature are
  themselves retrofits of existing lessons, not from-scratch builds.** If that's what
  the practice actually looks like on the ground, treating retrofit as a failure state
  or a lesser form of UDL doesn't match the evidence for how UDL gets done.
- **CAST's stated reason for the preference is efficiency and effectiveness, not
  outcome quality.** Nothing in the sourced material claims that a retrofit-derived
  redesign underperforms a proactively designed one on learning outcomes; the claim is
  only that starting proactively costs less time and money than fixing something
  already built.
