# Learner Variability

## Summary

Learner variability is the premise the entire UDL framework rests on. CAST's own
framing is blunt: **"The average brain — and by extension, the average learner —
does not exist."** Every principle, guideline, and consideration in the Guidelines is
downstream of this one claim: that differences between learners (and within one
learner across tasks and contexts) are not noise to average out but the actual shape
of the population you are designing for.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this page when you need to explain *why* UDL asks for multiple means
rather than one well-optimized means: this is the justification underneath every
other node in the framework, not a design move in itself.

The diagnostic question this page answers is not "how do I act on variability." It
is "what is the actual empirical and rhetorical basis for treating variability as
systematic rather than random". That question matters because a large amount of
popular UDL language claims more precision here than CAST's own materials assert.

## How it works

CAST states the norm claim directly:

> "Individual variability is the norm in learning environments, and learners differ
> from one another and an individual learner differs over time depending on the task
> and context."

CAST goes further than "people are different." It frames variability as
**structured and directional**, not just present: research findings show learners
are "highly variable" in measurable ways, and CAST positions variability as the most
"widely replicated finding in educational research."

**On the folk-UDL terms.** Two phrases circulate constantly in practitioner
discussion of this idea, and neither is CAST doctrine:

- **"Jagged learning profile."** This term is widely used in education and
  disability advocacy to describe the idea that a learner has peaks and valleys of
  ability across domains rather than one flat skill level. **It does not appear in
  CAST's official materials as a formal concept.** It is folk-UDL, a real and
  useful description of the same underlying phenomenon CAST calls variability, but
  not CAST's own vocabulary or a citable CAST construct.
- **"Designing for the edges."** CAST endorses the underlying principle: designing
  for learners at the margins tends to benefit everyone (the same logic as the
  curb-cut effect; see [[universal-design-origins]]), but the specific phrase
  "designing for the edges" does not appear in CAST's foundational
  definitions. CAST's own documents reference "design to the edges" as something
  closer to an observed outcome than a formally named principle.

Both terms are common enough, and useful enough, that this page is not warning you
away from them. It is warning you against citing them *as CAST's framework* when
what you mean is the practitioner gloss on it.

## In practice

When you use "learner variability" to justify a design decision, be precise about
which claim you are actually making:

1. **The weak claim** (well-supported): learners differ from each other, and any
   one learner's performance differs across tasks and contexts. This is close to
   descriptive fact and is not seriously contested.
2. **The strong claim** (CAST's actual framing): this variability is *systematic
   and predictable*: differences cluster in ways design can anticipate in advance,
   which is why proactive design ([[proactive-design-vs-retrofit]]) works better
   than after-the-fact accommodation.
3. **The folk claim** (not CAST doctrine, but common in practice): individual
   learners have a "jagged profile" of strengths and weaknesses that a teacher
   should map and design around directly.

A worked pass: a teacher says "my class is so mixed-ability, UDL is exactly what I
need." That's the weak claim, correctly applied: mixed performance is variability.
If they then say "so I need to find each kid's jagged profile and build to it,"
they've reached for folk-UDL language to describe something closer to
differentiated instruction than to UDL's actual move, which is to build flexible
options into the design up front rather than map individuals and target them. See
[[confusing-udl-with-differentiated-instruction]].

## Gotchas & constraints

- **"Variability is predictable" is a strong empirical claim, and the source
  grounding this page asserts it more than it demonstrates it.** CAST calls
  variability the most "widely replicated finding in educational research," which
  is a claim about the *existence and size* of individual differences, well
  established in psychometrics and educational measurement generally. It is a
  separate and stronger claim that this variability is *structured in ways that
  make specific instructional design responses predictably effective*. The staged
  source does not cite a study establishing the latter; it asserts CAST's framing.
  This gap in the source is disclosed here rather than papered over. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for what the outcomes literature does and
  does not show.
- **"Jagged learning profile" and "designing for the edges" are folk-UDL, not CAST
  doctrine.** Say this every time you encounter the terms used as if they were
  citable CAST concepts. They aren't, per the staged source's direct check
  against CAST's official materials.
- **The origin of the norm-claim is architecture, not neuroscience.** Learner
  variability as a design premise predates and is separate from the three-networks
  neuroscience framing; see [[the-three-brain-networks]] for that (contested)
  strand and [[universal-design-origins]] for the architectural lineage.
