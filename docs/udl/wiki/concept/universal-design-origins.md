# Universal Design Origins

## Summary

UDL's name and founding philosophy come from architecture, not education. Ronald
Mace, an architect and wheelchair user, coined "universal design" in the 1980s;
CAST, founded in 1984 by David Rose and Anne Meyer, explicitly adapted his
philosophy to curriculum starting around 1990. The adaptation carries a disanalogy
that the name itself obscures: architecture's universal design converges on *one*
artifact that works for everyone, while UDL produces *flexible options*, not a
single universal lesson. That difference is the most important thing to understand
about why "universal" is a misleading word for what UDL actually asks you to build.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this page when someone expects UDL to produce a single design that works
for all learners the way a curb cut works for all pedestrians, and you need to
explain why that expectation is set up by the name but not delivered by the
framework.

The diagnostic question: **when someone says "make it universal," do they mean
"build one thing that works for everyone" (architecture's sense) or "build several
paths to the same goal" (UDL's actual sense)?** These are different design tasks,
and the name "Universal Design for Learning" invites the first reading while CAST's
practice delivers the second.

## How it works

**Ron Mace and the architectural origin.** Ronald Lawrence Mace (1942–1998) was an
architect and wheelchair user who coined the term "universal design," defined as
"the design of products and environments to be usable by all people, to the
greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialized design."
He founded the Center for Universal Design at North Carolina State University in
1989, and the seven principles of universal design were developed by his working
group in 1997.

**CAST's adaptation, from 1984.** David Rose and Anne Meyer founded CAST (then
the Center for Applied Special Technology) in 1984. By 1990, CAST's stated goal
had shifted from addressing "the disabilities of the individual" to addressing
"the disabilities of schools," explicitly mirroring Mace's architectural
reframing (see [[barriers-and-learner-environment-mismatch]] for where that
reframing leads). Rose and Meyer are the principal architects of the UDL
framework built on that borrowed premise.

**The disanalogy CAST doesn't state outright.** CAST's own materials do not
explicitly spell out the distinction between how universal design works in
architecture and how it works in learning. It has to be inferred from how the
framework actually operates:

- **Architecture:** one well-designed building (a ramp, a curb cut, a lever
  door handle) serves everyone. The artifact is singular; the design work is
  finished once that one artifact exists.
- **UDL:** multiple pathways, materials, methods, and assessments all lead to the
  same rigorous learning goal. There is no single "universal lesson." The design
  work produces a set of *options*, and the learner's job (and the framework's
  actual mechanism) is selecting or being offered the fitting one from that set.

This is why "universal" is a misleading word for a novice to hear cold. It sounds
like it promises the education equivalent of a curb cut: build it once, correctly,
and the access problem is solved for everyone at once. What CAST actually built
is a method for generating flexible options, not a method for converging on one
design. The word survives from Mace's usage; the underlying design object does not
match.

## In practice

When applying UDL to a lesson or assessment, do not look for "the one design that
works for everyone": that goal doesn't exist in this framework and chasing it
will make you conflate UDL with something closer to universal design in the
architectural, single-artifact sense. Instead:

- Identify the firm goal (the construct), then generate two or three *different*
  means of reaching it: this is the actual output, and it stays plural. See
  [[firm-goals-flexible-means]] for the mechanics.
- If you find yourself iterating toward a single "best" version of an assignment
  that you think will work for the whole class, that is normal instructional
  design, not the distinctive move UDL is asking for. The distinctive move is
  keeping the options open rather than converging.
- When you want the curb-cut logic specifically ("design for people at the
  margins and everyone benefits"), that principle is present in CAST's framing
  in spirit, but treat it as an inherited justification from the architecture
  lineage rather than a formally cited CAST doctrine (see Gotchas).

## Gotchas & constraints

- **The curb-cut effect is endorsed in principle but not formally attributed in
  CAST's own materials.** CAST's documents reference "design to the edges" as an
  outcome concept (the idea that designing for learners at the margins benefits
  everyone), but the specific "curb-cut effect" framing, common in disability
  advocacy generally, is not formally cited or sourced back to Mace's work inside
  CAST's own published definitions. The lineage is real and well documented
  historically; the formal doctrinal link inside CAST's materials is thinner than
  the popular narrative suggests.
- **The architecture/UDL disanalogy above is this page's inference, not a quoted
  CAST position.** CAST's own documents do not explicitly state the
  one-artifact-versus-flexible-options distinction. It is a reasonable and
  necessary reading of how the two fields' design outputs actually differ, drawn
  from comparing Mace's definition of universal design against how CAST's
  Guidelines actually function (multiple means, not one form), but it should be
  presented as analysis, not as a CAST quotation.
- **"Jagged learning profile" and "designing for the edges" are folk-UDL terms
  that often travel alongside this origin story; neither is in CAST's formal
  published definitions.** See [[learner-variability]] for the direct treatment.
- **1984 is CAST's founding year, not the year the UDL framework or the term
  "Universal Design for Learning" itself was published.** The staged source
  supports the founding date and the 1990 reframing but does not give a specific
  date for when "Universal Design for Learning" became the framework's formal
  name: that gap is disclosed rather than filled in.
