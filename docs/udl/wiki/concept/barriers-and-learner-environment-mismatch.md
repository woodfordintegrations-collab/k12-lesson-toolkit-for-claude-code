# Barriers and the Learner–Environment Mismatch

## Summary

This is CAST's social-model commitment: a barrier to learning is a property of the
curriculum or environment, not a deficit inside the learner. CAST states this in its
starkest possible form: **"Curricula, rather than learners, are disabled."** It
reframes disability from individual pathology to a mismatch between a variable
learner and an inflexible design, and it is the doctrinal foundation for every
"remove the barrier" move elsewhere in the framework.

Reach for this page when you need the theoretical justification for redesigning the
environment instead of remediating the learner, or when you're evaluating whether
a UDL claim has overreached into territory the framework can't actually cover.

The diagnostic question is not "is this barrier real." CAST's model assumes most
instructional barriers are real and locatable in the design. It is "**does removing
this design barrier fully resolve the access problem, or does an individual
learner still need something design alone cannot supply?**" This page states the
doctrine; the second half states its limit.

## How it works

CAST's own statement of the model:

> "UDL aims to change the design of the environment rather than to situate the
> problem as a perceived deficit within the learner."

And, more pointedly:

> "Curricula, rather than learners, are disabled."

This is an explicit break from the medical model, where disability is treated as a
property located in an individual's body or mind that needs remediation or
accommodation. CAST's ecological/environmental model instead treats disability as
relational: a mismatch between what a learner brings and what a fixed design
demands of them. CAST states it directly: **"The environment in determining who is
or who is not considered 'disabled' plays the central role."**

Guidelines 3.0 (2024) widens this further. It extends the barrier concept beyond
individual instructional obstacles to "barriers rooted in biases and systems of
exclusion for learners with and without disabilities": meaning barriers can be
cultural and representational, not only mechanical (can this learner physically use
this material) or cognitive (can this learner decode this text).

## In practice

The mismatch model changes where you look when something isn't working. Practically:

- A student "can't focus" is, under this model, first a question about the
  environment (Is there unmanaged noise? Is the task poorly scaffolded? Is the
  content irrelevant to them?) before it is a question about the student.
- A worked pass: a student consistently fails word-problem sections of a math
  assessment but does well on computation. The medical-model read is "this student
  has a reading deficit." The mismatch read asks what the assessment's design is
  demanding that isn't part of the actual construct: if the goal is measuring
  math reasoning, decoding-heavy phrasing is a design barrier introducing
  construct-irrelevant variance, not evidence of a learner deficit. See
  [[firm-goals-flexible-means]] and [[construct-irrelevant-variance]] for the
  mechanism that makes this distinction precise rather than rhetorical.
- This doctrine is the reason UDL asks you to anticipate barriers *before*
  building a lesson rather than patch them after a student struggles. See
  [[proactive-design-vs-retrofit]].

## Gotchas & constraints

- **This is CAST doctrine, stated in CAST's own words, and it is also a strong
  and contestable claim.** "Curricula, rather than learners, are disabled" is a
  clean rhetorical formulation, but disability-studies scholars and disabled
  students themselves have pushed back on treating it as complete. The strongest
  version of this critique: framing disability as purely a design-mismatch problem
  risks **stigmatization and erasure**: if barriers are "inherently" removable
  through good design, some scholars argue, disabled students may face pressure to
  no longer self-identify as disabled or to stop requesting individualized
  accommodations, when in practice accommodations and disclosure remain necessary
  even in well-designed courses. A page presenting only CAST's doctrine would be
  advocacy, not a reference; that critique is recorded here because the source
  base includes it directly.
- **The evidence for "individualized support remains necessary" is documented,
  not merely asserted.** Research on UDL in higher education finds that even where
  UDL is implemented, students report continuing to face the administrative and
  advocacy burden of disclosure, and that accommodations must still be selected
  per learner because there is no one-size-fits-all design solution. This is a
  direct limit on how far the mismatch model can be taken in practice. See
  [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]] for the trap this produces when the
  doctrine gets over-applied.
- **The legal floor is a different thing entirely.** The mismatch model is a
  design philosophy, not an enforceable standard. Section 504, the ADA, and WCAG
  set testable accessibility requirements independent of whether an environment
  was "UDL-designed." Treating UDL implementation as equivalent to legal
  accessibility compliance is a distinct and separately documented error: see
  [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].
