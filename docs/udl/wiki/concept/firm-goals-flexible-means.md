# Firm Goals, Flexible Means

## Summary

Firm goals, flexible means is the operating move underneath every other page in this
wiki. Separate the construct being learned (the goal) from the vehicle used to reach or
demonstrate it (the means). Hold the goal fixed and rigorous. Vary the means as widely
as the learners in front of you require. Every consideration in the framework is this
same move, applied to one specific point in a lesson, an assessment, or a standard.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this page before any consideration page, whenever you're deciding what is
allowed to flex. It's the move that decides what "flexible" is permitted to touch.

| The actual problem | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| You already have a clean, construct-relevant goal and need specific design options | The relevant consideration page (7.1, 5.1, 3.2, etc.) |
| You need the measurement-theory version of this idea, with validity language | [[construct-irrelevant-variance]] |
| You're staring at a standard whose verb embeds a method | [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]] |
| You're worried a proposed change loosens the goal rather than the means | [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]] |
| You're retrofitting a lesson that already exists rather than designing one fresh | [[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]] |

The diagnostic question is not "can I offer options here?" It is "which part of this is
the thing I'm actually trying to measure or teach, and which part is just the vehicle I
happened to pick?" Get that wrong and every downstream design decision inherits the
error: you'll widen the wrong thing, or narrow the right one.

## How it works

CAST states the move directly: "The goal is achieved by keen focus on the goal, as
distinct from the means, enabling the provision of supports and scaffolds for construct
irrelevant items." A construct is the knowledge, skill, or ability actually being taught
or assessed. Anything else riding along with it, that a learner has to clear without it
being part of what's being measured, is construct-irrelevant.

CAST's own framing of the stakes: "Construct-irrelevant features in assessments can
create barriers that prevent some learners from accurately demonstrating what they have
learned." The reframe is deliberate. A barrier is not a learner shortfall. It's a design
choice that bundled the construct together with something that was never part of it.

Applied to standards, the same move gives you two rough categories. **This specific
schema (the two categories, their verb lists, and the diagnostic question below) is
Novak Education's operationalization of the CAST principle, not CAST doctrine itself.**
CAST's own guidance states the underlying principle (focus on the learning goal to
identify construct-irrelevant barriers); Novak Education, a commercial UDL consultancy,
is the source for this particular two-category framing. **Content standards**
demonstrate knowledge, and use verbs like explain, demonstrate, describe, analyze.
**Method standards** demonstrate skill at doing a specific thing, and use verbs like
write, use, measure, solve. The diagnostic question for reading any standard, from
Novak Education: "Is this standard requiring that students all do the exact same thing
in the same way?" If yes, the method is embedded in the goal, and the method is itself
part of the construct. If no, flexibility is already available and the means can be
varied freely.

## In practice

**A worked pass.** Take a standard as written: "Write a paragraph with topic sentence,
supporting details, and conclusion." Read literally, the method is writing, and a
student who can't produce a written paragraph looks like they haven't met the standard.

Apply the move. Ask what's actually being taught: understanding of paragraph structure
and composition, meaning the ability to state a claim, support it, and close it. That's
the construct. Once it's named, the flexible demonstration methods fall out on their
own: a written essay, a video explanation of the structure, an oral presentation with a
visual organizer, a digital interactive diagram. All of them can show whether a student
understands topic sentence, support, and conclusion. None of them requires handwriting
to prove it.

This does not eliminate standards that genuinely require a specific skill. If
handwriting itself, or a particular writing format, or a specific communication style,
is what's being taught, then that is the construct, not an incidental method for
reaching something else. A student still learns it. The move only applies once you've
correctly identified which case you're in, and that judgment call is the hard part.

A second, CAST-native example makes the same point at the level of a whole assessment.
"A mathematics assessment should measure mathematics problem-solving (the goal), not
reading proficiency (a construct-irrelevant barrier)." Multiple ways to present the
problem, respond to it, or demonstrate understanding all keep the goal firm while making
the means flexible. The reading demand was never the point. It just came along for the
ride.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **This is what makes UDL not a lowering of expectations.** CAST requires the goal to
  stay "specific" and "challenging" throughout. Flexibility is licensed for means,
  never for the goal itself. A design choice that widens the goal rather than the means
  is a different move entirely, and it fails for a different reason. See
  [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]].
- **Getting the goal wrong makes every downstream decision wrong.** Misidentify the
  construct as incidental and you'll strip out a real desirable difficulty, and stop
  measuring what you meant to measure. Make the opposite error, treating the actual
  method as the construct, and you'll leave a barrier in place that never needed to be
  there.
- **CAST does not supply an algorithm for telling the two apart.** Novak Education
  offers a heuristic question ("Is this standard requiring that students all do the
  exact same thing in the same way?"), not a decision procedure, and the heuristic is
  Novak's own, not CAST's. Some standards are
  genuinely hard cases, and the honest answer is sometimes "this requires judgment,
  not a formula."
- **The line moves with grade level and unit.** "Write legibly" is the construct in a
  handwriting-instruction unit and construct-irrelevant in a persuasive-argument unit.
  The same verb can sit on either side depending on what the goal actually is that
  week.
