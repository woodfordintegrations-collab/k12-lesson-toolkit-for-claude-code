# Is UDL the Learning-Styles Hypothesis?

## Summary

The charge is that [[multiple-means-of-representation]] is the discredited learning-styles
hypothesis under a new name. The frameworks are genuinely different claims, and the
difference is not cosmetic: UDL does not sort learners into modality types, and the
sorting step is what the learning-styles hypothesis needs to be true. But the strongest
published version of the critique does not rest on the two being identical. It rests on
something narrower and harder to dislodge: the representation principle has no outcome
evidence of its own. UDL as a whole framework does have one: **g = 0.43, 95% CI
[0.19, 0.67], k = 20** against business-as-usual controls (King-Sears et al., 2023). But a
pooled effect over whole-framework interventions is not a test of the claim that making
information perceptible through multiple modalities increases learning. That charge
survives the distinction intact.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this page when someone with research training has just encountered UDL and
recognised the shape of something they know failed. That reaction is well-founded and
should not be talked out of them. But the objection has two separable halves, and
answering the wrong one is why most treatments of this question are useless.

| The actual question | Where to go |
|---|---|
| Is UDL the same claim as learning styles? | Here. Answer: no. |
| Does UDL improve learning outcomes? | [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]]. The substantive question, the one the distinction does not answer, and the authority on the pooled literature. This page defers to it on every number |
| Is the brain-networks story real neuroscience? | [[the-neuroscience-basis-claim]]. A separate overclaim with a separate literature |
| Can "UDL was implemented" even be measured? | [[measuring-udl-implementation-fidelity]] |
| Isn't this just differentiated instruction? | [[confusing-udl-with-differentiated-instruction]] |
| Does federal law's "scientifically valid" settle it? | [[scientifically-valid-the-statutory-claim]]. It does not |

The failure mode this page exists to prevent: using the (correct) theoretical distinction
to wave away the (also correct) evidence critique. They are independent findings and both
hold.

## The claim

Two claims are in play and they need to be kept apart.

**The meshing hypothesis**, the falsifiable core of learning styles. In its standard
formulation from the psychological literature: *a learner has a stable preferred modality,
and instruction delivered in that modality produces better learning than instruction
delivered in a mismatched modality.* This is an interaction claim. It predicts a crossover:
visual learners should beat auditory learners on visually-presented material and lose to
them on auditorily-presented material. That specific prediction is what the literature
failed to support, and it is the reason learning styles is described as discredited.

**UDL's representation claim**, as CAST states it at Guideline 1
([[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] sits under it):

> Ensure "key information is equally perceptible to all learners by: 1) offering the same
> information through different modalities (e.g., through vision, hearing, or touch);
> 2) offering information in a format that will allow for adjustability by the user
> (e.g., text that can be enlarged, sounds that can be amplified); and 3) offering a
> variety of perspectives."

This is a main-effect claim about removing a gate, not an interaction claim about matching
a type. It says access should not be conditional on any single channel working for you.

**Who makes the identity charge.** Guy Boysen and Kathryn Ritchey, in *Lessons (Not)
Learned: The Troubling Similarities Between Learning Styles and Universal Design for
Learning*, *Scholarship of Teaching and Learning in Psychology* 8(1), 52–64 (2022). Their
claim is not that the two frameworks are the same claim. It is that they are similar in
three specific respects, one of which is decisive.

**Disclosure on the learning-styles side of the ledger.** This wiki's staged sources are
the UDL critique literature, which treats learning styles as settled-discredited
background and does not restage the primary evidence. No meta-analysis of the meshing
hypothesis was retrieved for this page. The word "discredited" here is carried from
Boysen & Ritchey's framing, not independently verified against the learning-styles
literature. A reader who wants that evidence should go to it directly.

## What the evidence shows

### 1. The frameworks are distinguishable in principle, and the distinction is real

The meshing hypothesis has a load-bearing step: classify the learner. Without a stable
per-learner modality type there is nothing to mesh instruction *to*. CAST's own doctrine
denies exactly that step:

> "Individual variability is the norm in learning environments, and learners differ from
> one another and an individual learner differs over time depending on the task and
> context."

A construct that varies *within* a learner across task and context is not a trait, and
cannot serve as the matching variable. [[learner-variability]] as CAST defines it is
formally incompatible with the premise the meshing hypothesis requires. The same holds for
the barrier doctrine ("UDL aims to change the design of the environment rather than to
situate the problem as a perceived deficit within the learner", and "Curricula, rather
than learners, are disabled"), which locates the object of design in the material, not in
a learner attribute (see [[barriers-and-learner-environment-mismatch]]).

This shows up in the design options themselves. Reading CAST's full published list for
Consideration 1.2, every option is of the form *provide X alongside Y*: descriptions for
all images, captions or speech-to-text for audio, transcripts for video, tactile
equivalents, sign languages. Not one instructs the educator to determine a learner's
modality and route them to it. The classification move is absent from the doctrine, not
merely de-emphasised.

**What each claim would take to falsify**, the cleanest way to see they are different
propositions:

| | Learning styles (meshing) | UDL representation |
|---|---|---|
| Design that tests it | Modality × learner-type factorial; look for crossover interaction | Comparison of UDL-designed instruction against business-as-usual on learning outcomes |
| Falsified by | Absence of the crossover interaction | Null main effect on learning |
| Requires classifying learners | Yes, necessarily | No |

Different designs, different failure conditions. They are not the same hypothesis, and a
result on one is not a result on the other.

### 2. The strongest critique survives that distinction anyway

Boysen & Ritchey's argument does not depend on the identity claim, which is why point 1
does not dispose of it:

> "UDL shares problematic similarities in theory, operationalization, and research with
> the discredited concept of learning styles. No strong research evidence exists that
> either approach increases learning. Both learning styles and UDL lack evidence showing
> that their implementation increases student learning, which is noted as the most
> important similarity between the two approaches."

The third similarity is the one that matters and it is not a similarity of content. It is
a similarity of evidential position. UDL's representation claim can be a perfectly
different proposition from the meshing hypothesis and still be untested, and the staged
sources say it is.

**Read the scope of that carefully, because this page has been wrong about it before.**
What has no evidence of its own is the *representation principle specifically*, the claim
that making information perceptible through multiple modalities increases learning. UDL as
a whole framework is not in that position: it has a pooled achievement estimate against
controls, staged in section 3. Boysen & Ritchey's quoted sentence is broader than what
this wiki's own sources will carry, and the narrower version is the one to hold.

Murphy's parallel statement:

> "No rigorous published research has demonstrated any improvement in an education
> intervention designed with UDL principles in mind. Because the effectiveness of this
> theory has not been proven, there are no grounds for UDL implementation plans to be
> framed as 'evidence-based' decisions."

Michael P. A. Murphy, *Belief Without Evidence? A Policy Research Note on Universal
Design for Learning*, *Policy Futures in Education* 19(1), 12–27 (2021).

**Date that quote before you use it.** Murphy's statement predates King-Sears et al.
(2023) and, read as written, is no longer accurate about the pooled achievement
literature. It remains accurate about the citation base behind the framework document,
which is what Boysen re-examined a year later.
[[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] carries the correction in full. Quoting Murphy bare,
as a live verdict on whether any UDL outcome evidence exists, is a dating error, and it is
the specific error this page used to make.

An audit of CAST's own citation base reaches the same place from inside the framework: the
cited studies "provided little evidence for claims about UDL, most studies did not offer a
choice to learners or measure learning, and none were related to brain function"
(attributed in the staged source to Ritchey and collaborators, reported via systematic
reviews 2023–2024 rather than fetched directly).

### 3. The counter-evidence, with its caveats

Two meta-analyses are staged and they are not of equal weight. The strong one is real and
must be stated first.

**King-Sears et al. (2023).** *Achievement of learners receiving UDL instruction: A
meta-analysis*, *Teaching and Teacher Education* 122, 103956. Verbatim from the staged
source: **g = 0.43, 95% CI [0.19, 0.67]**, over **k = 20** studies, with the design
recorded as "UDL-based interventions vs. business-as-usual control conditions, academic
achievement as outcome". A controlled comparison, a pooled achievement effect of roughly
two-fifths of a standard deviation, an interval that excludes zero. The sibling page calls
it "the strongest number in this literature and it is a real one", and that is the right
reading of it.

**What it reaches, and what it does not.** It reaches UDL-as-implemented. Twenty studies
of whole-framework interventions produce an average over a bundle: engagement moves,
action-and-expression moves, representation moves, plus small-group arrangement and
technology, pooled together. It cannot tell you that making information perceptible
through multiple modalities is the part that moved achievement, and the meta-analysts name
that gap themselves among their limitations: "limited specificity on which UDL principles
or combinations drive effects". So the framework has a number and the representation
principle does not. Both facts are staged in the same source and neither cancels the
other.

**Almeqdad, Alodat et al. (2023),** the weak entry. *Cogent Education* 10(1),
ERIC EJ1393594: empirical peer-reviewed pre- and post-design studies published 2015–2021
in English and Arabic, **k = 13**, across six countries, K-12 and higher education.
Verbatim:

> "The meta-analysis findings showed that the total effect sizes for the identified
> studies were 3.56; however, considerable heterogeneity was evident."

**A notation warning, because the staged sources are not consistent with each other.** The
critique extract writes the study count as "(N = 13)" and its appraisal as "N=13 studies",
while `sources/evidence-meta-analyses.md` records the same figure as "k (number of
studies): 13 studies" with participant N "Not specified". Thirteen is the number of
*studies*. This page uses **k = 13** in its own prose and preserves the "N" only inside
quoted material. An earlier version of this page reproduced the source's ambiguous
notation as a claim about participants; it was wrong.

**Confidence intervals for the 3.56 were not retrieved,** and the metric itself is
unidentified: the staged meta-analyses source flags it as appearing to be "a sum or
aggregated metric rather than a standardized Cohen's *d* or Hedges' *g*". Do not set 3.56
beside 0.43. They are not in the same units, and the larger number is the less
interpretable one.

The staged appraisal of that number is that effect sizes were "inflated by the use of
predominantly one-group pre-post designs without control groups, which are known to
overestimate effects", that "N=13 studies is very small for a meta-analysis", and that
"most did not measure learning outcomes directly; many measured perception or access
rather than achievement". A pooled figure of 3.56 from uncontrolled pre-post designs is
not counter-evidence to anything; it is uninterpretable rather than large. The fuller
treatment of both entries, and the authority on this literature, is
[[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]].

### 4. How CAST distinguishes the two, and what that is worth

The position recorded in the staged critique source, attributed to CAST and allied UDL
literature rather than traced to a specific fetched CAST page:

> "While the premise of UDL sounds similar to learning styles theory, the central idea of
> learning styles also proposes that education should match the diverse ways that students
> learn. However, UDL differs in that UDL dictates that instructors should provide students
> with multiple modes for consuming information, demonstrating learning, and engaging with
> learning experiences, allowing students to select the material that best allows them to
> learn."

That is the right distinction, the learner selects rather than being assigned, and it
matches CAST's published doctrine. It is also a theoretical distinction only. The staged
source's own appraisal is blunt about this: "ACKNOWLEDGED BUT NOT CONVINCING... CAST does
distinguish UDL from learning styles on theoretical grounds (universal design vs. trait
matching), but does not address Boysen & Ritchey's core empirical claim: that both
frameworks lack evidence of improved learning. The distinction does not resolve the
evidence gap."

Winning the definitional argument produces no data. Nothing in the distinction supplies an
outcome study, and no new empirical evidence is cited in support of the claim that the
distinction matters for learning outcomes.

**Verdict.** UDL's representation principle is *not* the meshing hypothesis: it makes no
per-learner modality classification, its central variability doctrine explicitly denies the
stable trait such classification would need, and it would be tested by a different design
against a different null. The identity charge fails. But the acquittal is narrow and buys
the representation principle almost nothing, because the serious version of the critique
never required the identity. Stated at the altitude the evidence will actually bear:
**UDL as a whole framework has a moderate pooled achievement effect against
business-as-usual controls (g = 0.43, 95% CI [0.19, 0.67], k = 20; King-Sears et al.,
2023). The representation principle specifically has no outcome evidence of its own.**
Those two are both true and they are not in tension. A pooled estimate over
whole-framework interventions cannot identify which principle moved the outcome, and the
meta-analysts say so themselves: "limited specificity on which UDL principles or
combinations drive effects". The honest summary is that UDL is not learning styles, the
framework has a real number, and the specific mechanism this page is about,
perceptibility through multiple modalities, has never been isolated and tested. That is
where it shares learning styles' position, and only there.

**Settled, on these sources:** two claims, and they have to be held together.
(1) The theoretical distinction is real and grounded in CAST's published text.
(2) The **representation principle specifically** has no outcome evidence of its own: no
staged source isolates multiple means of representation and measures learning against a
control. The distinction and the critique's evidential charge are both marked WELL-SOURCED
in the staged critique file (Boysen & Ritchey 2022; Murphy 2021, with the dating caveat
above; the CAST citation audit), and the narrowing in (2) is this wiki's own, forced by
King-Sears et al. (2023), whose stated limitation ("limited specificity on which UDL
principles or combinations drive effects") is the same finding read from the other side.

**Not settled, and previously asserted here in error:** that UDL lacks learning-outcome
evidence. It does not. King-Sears et al. (2023) report **g = 0.43, 95% CI [0.19, 0.67],
k = 20** against business-as-usual controls, and [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] is
the authority on that literature. This page's settled claim is about the mechanism, not
the framework, and it must not be quoted as the broader one.

**Contested:** whether the distinction survives contact with classroom practice (see
below); whether the Almeqdad et al. (2023) meta-analysis constitutes counter-evidence at
all; what the King-Sears pooled effect is an effect *of*, given that the 20 studies do not
share an operationalization of "UDL"; and the status of "discredited" for learning styles
itself, which this page inherits rather than verifies.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **The blurred boundary is a practice problem, not a theory problem, and it is common.**
  A teacher who says *"Maya is a visual learner, so I'll give her the diagram"* is doing
  learning styles while sincerely believing they are doing UDL. Three moves happen in that
  sentence and none of them is UDL: the learner is **classified** into a modality type, a
  single representation is **assigned** to her, and the other representations are
  **withheld** from her. The UDL version of the same lesson is that the diagram, the text,
  and the narration are all available to everyone, Maya chooses among them, and she may
  choose the text. **Offering an option is not the same act as assigning one.** The
  distinction lives entirely in whether the learner or the teacher does the selecting, and
  in whether the unchosen options remain available.
- **UDL practice does slide toward matching, and there is a seam in CAST's own text where
  the slide starts.** At the principle level CAST writes that there is "not one means of
  representation that will be optimal for every learner", which is per-learner optimality
  language that reads naturally as trait-matching. At the guideline level the framing is universal:
  information "equally perceptible to all learners". The second is the defensible claim;
  the first is the one practitioners quote. Consideration 1.1 compounds it by describing
  display settings adjusted to "needs and preferences", and *preference* is the exact
  vocabulary learning styles trades in, though 1.1's actual content (font size, contrast,
  speech rate) is display adjustability, not modality selection. See
  [[customize-the-display-of-information]].
- **"It's just good teaching" is not a defence against this critique.** Boysen & Ritchey's
  charge is not that UDL's practices are harmful. Many of them are independently supported
  as instructional practices. The charge is about what is claimed *on UDL's behalf*, that
  designing this way improves learning, which is a claim about UDL as a framework, and
  which the individual practices' separate track records do not establish.
- **The critique literature predates Guidelines 3.0.** Boysen & Ritchey is 2022; Murphy is
  2021; UDL 3.0 was released 2024-07-30. These papers analyse 2.2-era UDL and use 2.2
  vocabulary ("checkpoints", "provide options"). Nothing in the staged 3.0 material changes
  the structure of the representation claim, so the critique transfers. But the papers
  cannot be cited as having examined 3.0, and no post-3.0 peer-reviewed response from CAST
  to these critiques was located in the staged sources.
- **Do not run the argument backwards.** "UDL isn't learning styles" is true and is not a
  finding about effectiveness. Anyone who moves from the distinction to a conclusion about
  outcomes has changed the subject. That move is the specific reason most treatments of
  this question leave the reader worse informed than before.
