# Can UDL Implementation Be Measured?

## Summary

Instruments that score UDL implementation exist, and one of them reports strong
reliability statistics. Reliability was never the hard question. Whether "this
classroom implements UDL" names a determinate property, one an instrument could be
*valid for*, is the hard question, and no source staged for this wiki reports the
validity evidence that would settle it. This page sits upstream of every other evidence
claim here.

## When to reach for it

Read this before you do any of the following:

| What you are about to do | Why this comes first |
|---|---|
| Interpret a UDL outcome study | The independent variable is whatever that study called UDL |
| Pool or compare effect sizes across studies | The studies are not measuring the same treatment |
| Adopt a walkthrough tool for teacher evaluation | Scoring consistency is not scoring the right thing |
| Answer "are we doing UDL?" with a number | Ask first what the number is a number about |

This page is about measuring **implementation**. Nearby questions route elsewhere:

## The claim

Stated so it can fail:

> **UDL implementation is a measurable property of a classroom. There is a fact of the
> matter about whether UDL is present and to what degree, and two independently trained
> observers watching the same lesson would agree on it.**

Nobody argues for this in print; it is asserted by construction. Every instrument that
scores implementation presupposes it: the UDL Observation Measurement Tool (Basham,
Gardner & Smith), CAST's UDL Schoolwide Implementation Criteria, the CAST UDL
Progression Rubric, the walkthrough protocols. So does every outcome study whose
independent variable is "UDL instruction", and every policy directing schools to
implement UDL.

The claim has two falsifiers, and they are not the same test:

1. **The reliability falsifier.** Independently trained observers scoring the same
   lesson diverge. Testable, tested, and the claim survives. See below.
2. **The validity falsifier.** Instruments purporting to measure the same construct fail
   to converge; or the instrument fails to discriminate a UDL-designed lesson from a
   well-run lesson designed without it. Testable, and **not tested in any source staged
   for this wiki**.

Passing the first is what the literature has. The second is what the claim needs.

## What the evidence shows

### The instruments that exist

**UDL Observation Measurement Tool (UDL-OMT)**, the only instrument here with
published psychometrics. Origin: Basham, J. D., Gardner, J. E., & Smith, S. J. (2020),
an instrument field test. Its design is "structured observation rubrics aligned to
three UDL principles (Engagement, Representation, Action/Expression)". The staged source
records that it showed "good to excellent internal consistency", that it can
"characterize differences in UDL implementation across a continuum of settings", and
that it was "validated as formative evaluation tool for practitioners". The sample for
that reliability testing is recorded as "Not specified in available abstracts". Its own
stated limitations are the most useful thing about it:

> Design, flexibility, and iterative nature of UDL make consistent measurement
> difficult · Need for training and inter-rater reliability protocols · No consensus on
> fidelity thresholds

**The cross-cultural reliability study.** Rusconi, L., & Gäng-Pacifico, D. (2026),
*Frontiers in Education*. Design: cross-cultural adaptation and reliability study.
Sample: 29 video-recorded lessons (13 Italian, 16 French); 4 observers per language.
Verbatim from the staged extract:

*Internal consistency: Cronbach's Alpha*

| | Italian version | French version |
|---|---|---|
| Total Scale | α = 0.980 | α = 0.980 |
| Core Dimensions | α = 0.969 (A, B1, C1, D1) | α = 0.967 |
| Individual dimensions | 0.854–0.939 | 0.709–0.954 |

*Inter-rater reliability: Intraclass Correlation Coefficient*

- Italian version: **ICC = 0.73–0.94 (moderate to excellent)**
- French version: **ICC = 0.71–0.82 (moderate to very good)**

*Confidence intervals*, of which the source gives two examples only, not a full set:

- Italian Dimension C1: **[0.851, 0.978]**
- French Dimension A: **[0.588, 0.925]**

Stated key finding: "Satisfactory reliability even when used by evaluators with varying
levels of expertise."

**UDL Schoolwide Implementation Criteria (UDL-SIC)**, CAST's schoolwide tool, and a
**self-assessment**. Four domains, four elements each: "School Culture & Environment,
Teaching and Learning, Leadership and Management, and Professional Learning". Purpose,
verbatim: "designed to help school teams map their school's current UDL practices to
determine where they are and what next steps they can take on their UDL journey." A
paired Goal-Setting Planning Tool supports action planning. **No reliability or validity
statistics for the UDL-SIC appear in any source staged for this wiki.**

**CAST UDL Progression Rubric** (Novak & Rodriguez, CAST Professional Publishing): an
8-page PDF measuring educator progress in implementing the Guidelines. Scoring levels:
Emerging, Proficient, Progressing toward Expert Practice. Stated use cases:
"Self-evaluation by educators; team-level progress assessment." It is an implementation
rubric, not a curriculum-materials rubric. **No psychometrics reported.**

**Walkthrough and checklist tools.** An 8-focus-area observation protocol used in
walkthroughs as a "spot-it" tool, where "observers look for visible evidence of each item
rather than relying on teacher self-report", and which is framed to spark "coaching
conversations, not evaluative judgments". And a 12-item lesson checklist, "a quick 2-3
minute sanity check before delivering lessons", with the instruction that "Teachers
should aim for at least 8 of 12 items checked per lesson". These are practitioner and
vendor materials rather than CAST doctrine, and the 8-of-12 threshold has no derivation
anywhere in the source.

### What those reliability numbers do and do not establish

This is the point of the page.

α is internal consistency: items within a scale covary. The ICC here is inter-rater
agreement: different observers score the same lesson alike. Both are **reliability**
coefficients. Neither is a validity coefficient. Together they establish that the
UDL-OMT is a *consistent* instrument: trained observers apply the rubric the same way,
and the rubric's items hang together. That is a real achievement and it is not nothing.

What it does not establish:

- **That what the rubric scores is UDL.** Content validity requires a specification of
  the construct that the items were sampled from. The field's position on whether such a
  specification exists is reported below.
- **That the rubric's structure matches the framework's.** UDL 3.0 is a 3 × 3 grid:
  three principles crossed with the Access / Support / Executive Function rows, see
  [[access-support-executive-function]]. No factor analysis against that structure is
  reported.
- **That two instruments would agree.** No source staged here reports the UDL-OMT and
  the Progression Rubric, or any other pair, scored against the same classrooms.
  Convergent validity is simply absent.
- **That a high score means UDL rather than good teaching.** This is the discriminant
  question, and it is the one that matters. The implementation literature answers
  "isn't UDL just good teaching?" doctrinally, with "Good teaching isn't automatically
  universally designed—UDL requires strategic, intentional barrier removal", not with
  data. An instrument that cannot separate the two scores a well-run non-UDL lesson high.

Two further readings of the numbers themselves. First, α = 0.980 on the total scale in
*both* languages is not straightforwardly good news. At that magnitude items are close to
redundant; an alpha that high is ordinarily read as a scale measuring one narrow thing
many times, not a broad construct measured well. For an instrument whose construct spans
three principles and nine guidelines, near-unity total-scale α is a structural finding
that wants explaining, and the source does not explain it.

Second, the two reported intervals. French Dimension A, [0.588, 0.925], runs from
moderate to excellent, so the point estimate alone is not a defensible summary of that
dimension. With 29 lessons and 4 raters per language, that width is what you would
expect, and it is why the honest report of the ICC is a range, not a headline number.

The staged evidence summary itself draws the line correctly. Its characterization is
that these results establish "that UDL implementation *can* be reliably observed."
Reliably. Not validly. The distinction is preserved at the source and lost downstream.

### Operationalization: the field concedes the problem

Reviews converge on the same finding, and it is not a hostile one:

- Ok, Rao, Bryant & McDougall (2017), systematic review, k = 13. Stated limitations
  include "Lack of standard formats for describing UDL operationalization" and
  "**No consensus on how to measure UDL implementation.**"
- Rao, Ok & Bryant (2014): "Researchers report on their application of UD principles in
  varied ways, with no standard formats for describing how UD is used."
- King-Sears et al. (2023), the strongest meta-analysis in this wiki's sources, k = 20,
  overall combined effect **g = 0.43 [95% CI: 0.19, 0.67]**, lists among its *own*
  limitations "Variations in UDL implementation operationalization."
- Boysen (2024): "inadequate operationalization of UDL principles."
- The 2025 faculty-training review: "Questions remain about which core UDL components
  require strict fidelity vs. contextual adaptation."

This is conceded rather than merely alleged. The critique synthesis states it plainly:
"The definitional vagueness is not disputed even by UDL proponents; their response is
typically to call for better operationalization rather than to deny the problem."
Edyburn's framing is the cleanest statement of the dependency: "Knowing what UDL looks
like is an essential step in measuring the outcomes of UDL."

That dependency is why this page is upstream of the others. If "UDL instruction" is not a
determinate treatment, a pooled effect over studies that each called something different
UDL is not an estimate of one quantity. King-Sears et al. report five significant
moderators alongside "considerable heterogeneity in included studies"; with an undefined
independent variable, heterogeneity is the predicted result rather than a puzzle. Read
[[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] with that held in mind: the number there is real,
and what it is a number *about* is the open question.

### The self-report problem

Two of the four instrument families above are self-assessments: the UDL-SIC by design,
the Progression Rubric in its stated use cases. The walkthrough protocol defines itself
against exactly this, looking for "visible evidence of each item rather than relying on
teacher self-report". The implementation literature's own practice tools already treat
self-report as the weaker measure, and yet the schoolwide instrument a district is most
likely to adopt is the self-assessment. That bears directly on
[[implement-udl-at-school-scale]].

The professional-development literature inherits the problem in its outcome measures.
The staged finding that "spending more time on training had a significant effect on
long-term use of UDL" sits beside its own qualifier that "training duration did not
affect how much participants *felt* they learned about UDL", and the parallel finding
that trainees "were more capable of applying it in their work". Felt, reported, capable.
Self-reported implementation and observed implementation are different measurements of
different things, and no source staged here reports the correlation between them.

## Gotchas & constraints

**The deeper issue: UDL is a design stance, and a stance may be poorly suited to
observational checklists in the first place.**

The move UDL actually asks for happens before the lesson exists. You separate the goal
(the construct to be learned) from the means of reaching or demonstrating it, then vary
the means without touching the goal. That is [[firm-goals-flexible-means]], and its
rigour comes from [[construct-irrelevant-variance]]: the variation you remove has to be
the variation that is irrelevant to what you are measuring.

An observer at the back of the room sees the *output* of that decision. Options on offer,
formats available, choices presented. They cannot see the decision, and the decision is
what determines whether the options were the right ones, because the same array of
options is correct under one goal and construct-shifting under another. Two lessons can
be observationally identical and be, respectively, an instance of UDL and an instance of
[[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]]. No score computed from visible evidence
separates them, because the discriminating variable (what the goal actually was) is
not in the observer's field of view.

That is construct underrepresentation, not measurement error. It does not improve with
better rater training, and it is precisely the failure a high ICC conceals: the raters
agree because they are all scoring the same visible thing, and the visible thing is not
the construct.

The rest follows from it:

- **The observable half is the maximizable half.** An instrument that counts options
  rewards more options, and the framework says that is wrong: "UDL isn't 'just about
  giving students choice'." Deploy such an instrument as a fidelity measure and you
  create pressure toward [[choice-as-a-substitute-for-design]], the error the framework
  itself names. Instruments used for accountability change what happens in front of them.
- **There are no fidelity thresholds.** The UDL-OMT field test lists "No consensus on
  fidelity thresholds" among its own limitations, and the 8-of-12 checklist target has no
  standard-setting procedure behind it. A cut score without a derivation is a convention,
  not a criterion, and it cannot carry the claim that a classroom "implements UDL".
- **Version alignment is undisclosed.** The UDL-OMT field test is dated 2020, four years
  before Guidelines 3.0 (released 30 July 2024), which renamed nodes and restructured
  the second axis. Whether the tool or its Italian and French adaptations were re-aligned
  to 3.0 is not recorded in any source staged for this wiki. For an instrument whose
  content validity depends on matching the framework, that is not a footnote.
- **Generalization bound.** 29 video-recorded lessons, 4 observers per language, Italian
  and French. Video is not a live walkthrough, and raters in a reliability study are not
  administrators conducting evaluations. The coefficients are what they are; they are not
  a warrant for personnel evaluation.
- **None of this is legally testable.** A UDL-OMT score is not a WCAG 2.2 conformance
  result, and no instrument here tests accessibility. Those obligations have pass
  criteria and enforcement; implementation scores have neither. See
  [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].
- **Disclosed gap.** No source staged for this wiki reports a construct-validity study of
  any UDL implementation instrument: no factor structure, no convergent or discriminant
  evidence, no relation between instrument scores and student outcomes. The absence is
  the finding. Until it is filled, the defensible statement is narrow: UDL implementation
  can be *scored consistently*, and what the score means is unestablished.
