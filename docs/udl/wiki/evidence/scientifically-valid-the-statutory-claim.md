# "Scientifically Valid": What the Statute Does and Does Not Say

## Summary

US federal law defines Universal Design for Learning as "a scientifically valid
framework for guiding educational practice", at 20 U.S.C. § 1003(24), enacted 2008. That
phrase is quoted constantly, and it is usually quoted as though Congress had settled an
empirical question. It did not. A statutory definition fixes what a word means inside a
statute. It carries legal force and no evidentiary force at all.

## When to reach for it

Reach for this page when the sentence "UDL is defined in federal law as a scientifically
valid framework" is doing argumentative work: in a grant narrative, a district adoption
memo, a professional-learning deck, a vendor page, or a curriculum review. It is a true
sentence being used to answer a question it cannot answer.

It is the wrong page for several nearby questions:

| The actual question | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| Does UDL-designed instruction improve learning outcomes? | [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] |
| Can we even tell whether UDL was implemented? | [[measuring-udl-implementation-fidelity]] |
| Is the three-networks brain model sound? | [[the-neuroscience-basis-claim]] |
| Are our materials legally accessible? | [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]] |
| Is "multiple means of representation" learning styles again? | [[udl-and-the-learning-styles-problem]] |

The diagnostic question is narrow: **is the statute being cited to establish what UDL
means, or to establish that UDL works?** The first use is correct and useful. The second
is the error this page is about.

## The claim

**Claim under test:** that the description of UDL as "a scientifically valid framework"
in 20 U.S.C. § 1003(24) constitutes evidence that UDL is scientifically valid.

The claim is rarely stated this baldly. It appears as an implication: the phrase is
quoted, the citation is attached, and the reader is left to infer that a federal
determination has been made. Whoever quotes the phrase in support of UDL's
effectiveness is making the claim, whether or not they would defend it in that form.

It is falsifiable in a specific way: if the claim held, the statutory text would have to
be the product of an evidentiary process, and it would have to postdate the evidence it
summarizes. Neither is the case.

## What the evidence shows

### The statutory text, verbatim

The definition sits in the Higher Education Act of 1965 as amended by the **Higher
Education Opportunity Act, Public Law 110-315**, enacted **August 14, 2008**.

**Citation: 20 U.S.C. § 1003(24)** [Section 103 of the Higher Education Opportunity Act]

> "Universal design for learning" means a scientifically valid framework for guiding
> educational practice that (A) provides flexibility in the ways information is
> presented, in the ways students respond or demonstrate knowledge and skills, and in
> the ways students are engaged; and (B) reduces barriers in instruction, provides
> appropriate accommodations, supports, and challenges, and maintains high achievement
> expectations for all students, including students with disabilities and students who
> are limited English proficient.

That is the whole of it. Note what the definition contains: three dimensions
(presentation, response and demonstration, engagement), barrier reduction, maintained
high expectations, and an explicit population. Note what it does not contain: any
success criterion, any threshold, any test by which a given course could be shown to
satisfy it, and any reference to CAST's Guidelines.

### What a statutory definition is

A definition inside a statute is a **stipulation**. It fixes the meaning of a term for
the purposes of that statute so the operative provisions have determinate scope. It is
drafted by legislative staff, negotiated between offices and stakeholders, and enacted
by vote. At no point in that process is it tested.

This is not a defect in the drafting. It is what definitional sections are for, and
every statute has them. The consequence is simply that the words inside one describe a
legal object, not a measured one. A statute could define a triangle as having four
sides, and the only consequence would be legal: within that statute, four-sided things
would be triangles, and geometry would be untouched. "Scientifically valid" appearing
inside a definitional clause has exactly that character. It is a legislative
characterization of the thing being defined, and it takes its authority from enactment,
not from data.

The word "framework" in the same sentence does independent work here. Law and policy
describe UDL as a **framework** and a set of **principles**, not as a requirement or a
standard, and the distinction is precise. Frameworks guide decisions without dictating
outcomes; standards specify what must be achieved. The statute characterizes UDL as the
former in the same breath it calls it scientifically valid.

### The date does not work

The definition was enacted in **2008**. The meta-analytic and critical literature on UDL
largely postdates it. The 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Cogent Education*
examined studies published **between 2015 and 2021**. Murphy's policy critique is 2021;
Boysen and Ritchey's learning-styles paper is 2022. The 2008 drafters were not
condensing a body of effectiveness research, because in 2008 the body of effectiveness
research that is now argued over had not been produced.

Whatever the phrase "scientifically valid" was doing in 2008, it was not summarizing
findings that did not yet exist.

### What the effectiveness evidence actually shows

Set against the statutory phrase, the staged research record reports the following.

From Almeqdad et al.'s 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis (*Cogent Education*,
vol. 10, no. 1; ERIC EJ1393594), verbatim:

> The meta-analysis findings showed that the total effect sizes for the identified
> studies were 3.56; however, considerable heterogeneity was evident.

The same study is described as analysing **k = 13** empirical peer-reviewed studies of
pre- and post-design published 2015–2021 in English and Arabic, across six countries,
"mainly used one group quantitative research design". The 13 is a study count, not a
participant count: the staged critique extract itself reports it as "(N = 13)" in one
place, a notational slip this page does not repeat.

**The interval-bearing figure.** The 3.56 total carries no confidence interval in the
staged source, and the source itself flags the metric as ambiguous: it "appears to be a
sum or aggregated metric rather than a standardized Cohen's *d* or Hedges' *g*", with the
exact calculation method not retrieved. The comparable, standardized number is a separate
2023 meta-analysis, staged at `sources/evidence-meta-analyses.md`: King-Sears et al.
(*Teaching and Teacher Education*, 122, 103956) report, verbatim, an overall combined
effect of **g = 0.43, 95% CI [0.19, 0.67]**, pooling **k = 20** studies of UDL-based
instruction against business-as-usual control conditions. [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]]
is the page that appraises the outcome literature in full, including this figure's
subgroup breakdowns and limitations; treat its numbers as the authority and this one as a
pointer. It exists here only to make the narrower point that a 2023 empirical result
cannot have been summarized by 2008 statutory language.

The staged critique of that figure is that the design mix inflates it: predominantly
one-group pre-post studies without control groups are known to overestimate effects,
k = 13 is very small for a meta-analysis, and many of the included studies measured
perception or access rather than achievement.

Running underneath is the operationalization problem, which is the part that bears
directly on the word "valid":

> There have been ongoing critiques of UDL for lacking clarity in definition,
> challenges with implementation, and insufficient evidence of its effectiveness.
> Additionally, studies have pointed out the variability in definitions and
> implementation surrounding UDL... By revealing uneven application of UD principles and
> persistent conceptual ambiguity between UD and UDL, research clarifies why current
> evidence is difficult to compare and why causal claims about effectiveness remain
> weak.

Edyburn's framing is the compact version: knowing what UDL looks like is a precondition
for measuring its outcomes. Where the construct is not fixed, "scientifically valid" has
no determinate referent to be valid *about*. That is not a legal problem and the statute
does not create it, but it is the reason the statutory adjective cannot be borrowed as a
finding. See [[measuring-udl-implementation-fidelity]].

Murphy states the strong form of the critique:

> No rigorous published research has demonstrated any improvement in an education
> intervention designed with UDL principles in mind. Because the effectiveness of this
> theory has not been proven, there are no grounds for UDL implementation plans to be
> framed as 'evidence-based' decisions.

This page does not adjudicate Murphy against the meta-analysis. It observes only that
the question is live in the peer-reviewed literature, and that a live question is not
what a settled statutory phrase implies.

### What ESSA requires, precisely

ESSA (Public Law 114-95, 2015) is the first appearance of UDL in K-12 policy, and it is
routinely over-read. UDL appears in **two** places, both about assessment:

| Provision | What it says | Legal character |
|---|---|---|
| § 1111(b)(2)(B)(xiii) | States must develop assessments "to the extent practicable, using the principles of universal design for learning" | Qualified requirement. Applies to state academic assessments |
| § 1111(b)(2)(D)(iv) | States must describe "the steps the State has taken to incorporate universal design for learning, to the extent feasible, in alternate assessments" | Qualified requirement plus a reporting obligation. Applies to alternate assessments for students with significant cognitive disabilities |

Both are real obligations. Both are qualified: **"to the extent practicable"** in the
first, **"to the extent feasible"** in the second. States must take steps and document
them; the qualifier lets practical constraint be balanced against the principles.

What ESSA does **not** do, from the same source:

- It does not mandate UDL in classroom instruction or curriculum design.
- It does not establish enforcement mechanisms or penalties for non-compliance.
- It does not specify technical standards or implementation details.

And **IDEA does not mention UDL at all.** A search of the Individuals with Disabilities
Education Act and its implementing regulations at 34 CFR Part 300 finds no explicit
reference in the statutory text or the federal regulations. IDEA reaches the same
territory by a different mechanism: FAPE, the IEP, related services, and least
restrictive environment. UDL principles may inform IEP development, but that is guidance
rather than requirement.

So the accurate sentence is: *federal law requires states to use UDL principles in
assessment design where practicable, and requires nothing about UDL in teaching.* The
sentence people write instead, "ESSA requires UDL", is wrong in a way that matters,
because it converts an assessment-design qualifier into a classroom mandate that no
statute contains.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **This page is not an argument against UDL, and must not be read as one.** It is an
  argument against one citation practice: quoting a definitional clause as though it
  were a finding. Everything on this page would be equally true if UDL turned out to be
  the most effective framework in education. The statutory phrase would still be a
  stipulation, and it would still not be the reason to believe it.

- **The absence of statutory validation is not evidence of ineffectiveness.** Removing a
  bad argument for a claim leaves the claim exactly where the actual evidence puts it,
  which is [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]]. Do not run this page backwards.

- **The statutory definition is genuinely useful for what it is.** It is a stable,
  quotable, government-issued definition of UDL that funding language, board policy, and
  state plans can hang on. It has three further virtues worth naming. It predates the
  3.0 rewrite and survived it untouched: CAST's vocabulary changed substantially in
  July 2024, and the statute's wording did not, so a policy citing § 1003(24) does not
  go stale when the Guidelines are revised. Its three dimensions map onto the three
  principles without using any version-specific term. And it is short enough to quote in
  full, which is rare in education policy. Cite it for what UDL *means*. That is the job
  it can do.

- **The statute does not incorporate CAST's Guidelines by reference.** ESSA's "principles
  of universal design for learning" and § 1003(24)'s definition are abstract; neither
  points to the Guidelines document, and ESSA "does not specify technical standards or
  implementation details". A state can satisfy the assessment provision without adopting
  CAST's structure, and adopting the Guidelines 3.0 structure is not automatic compliance
  with anything.

- **Do not slide from this page into the accessibility line.** UDL's weak legal status
  is not shared by accessibility law. Section 504, ADA Title II, and the 2024 DOJ Title
  II rule (WCAG 2.0 Level AA) are enforceable, testable, and carry penalties. The
  statutory UDL definition does none of that, and neither substitutes for the other. See
  [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]].

- **Scope of the evidence reported here.** The 3.56 figure comes from the critique
  extract and has no confidence interval in that source. The King-Sears g = 0.43, 95% CI
  [0.19, 0.67], k = 20 figure comes from the meta-analytic extract
  (`sources/evidence-meta-analyses.md`), staged and quoted verbatim. Nothing here should
  be read as this wiki's complete appraisal of UDL's effect on outcomes. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for that.
