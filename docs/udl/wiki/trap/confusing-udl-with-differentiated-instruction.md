# UDL Is Not Differentiated Instruction

## Summary

Differentiated instruction (DI) and UDL are both responses to learner variability, and
that similarity is exactly what makes them easy to conflate. CAST is explicit that they
are not the same activity: DI is **reactive**: you notice a student struggling and
adjust; UDL is **proactive**: you design the environment so fewer of those reactive
adjustments are needed in the first place. The trap is not doing DI. DI is good,
evidence-based teaching and it is not going away. The trap is believing the DI you are
doing *is* UDL, which means the underlying design never gets rebuilt, and next year you
make the same adjustments to the same lesson for a new set of students who hit the same
barrier.

## When to reach for it

You are at risk of this trap the moment you catch yourself calling something "UDL" that
happened *after* you met the students, in response to one student's struggle, rather
than something built into the lesson before anyone walked in.

The tell: ask what changed in the design (the goal, the materials, the methods, the
assessment) as opposed to what changed for one student's experience of the lesson. If
the honest answer is "nothing changed in the design, I just helped one student
differently than the rest," you did DI. That's fine on its own terms. Calling it UDL on
a walkthrough form or in a grant report is the error.

| What's actually happening | Where this sits |
|---|---|
| You noticed a specific student struggling and adjusted on the spot | DI: legitimate, not this trap, but worth logging (see below) |
| The same barrier surfaces for several students across several lessons and you keep patching it individually | the trap: the pattern should have triggered a redesign, not more patches |
| You built the flexible option into the lesson before teaching it | real UDL |
| A student has a documented disability and needs an individualized adjustment regardless of how well the lesson is designed | [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]]: a different, ongoing case, not this trap |
| You're not sure whether an adjustment changed the construct itself | [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]] |

## How it works

CAST states the two definitions side by side, and the wording is worth reading closely:

> Differentiated Instruction: "Recognizing students' varying background knowledge,
> readiness, language, preferences in learning and interests; and to react
> responsively."

> Universal Design for Learning: "A theoretical framework to guide the design and
> development of learning environments that represent materials in flexible ways and
> offers a variety of options for learners to comprehend information, demonstrate their
> knowledge and skills, and be motivated to learn."

Everything hinges on one word pair: *react* versus *design*. DI's unit of action is the
individual student, discovered in the moment. UDL's unit of action is the environment,
built in advance. CAST's own comparison names the difference on every axis: timing
(reactive vs. proactive), scope (individual vs. universal), and goal (meet diverse needs
vs. eliminate barriers so fewer adaptations are needed).

The conflation is tempting for a real reason: both practices are motivated by the same
observation, that learners differ, and both make a teacher feel responsive and
attentive when done well. DI is not a lesser or unscientific practice; it is required (an
IEP is differentiation by definition) and it is often the correct immediate move for the
student in front of you right now. The error is not choosing DI. It is letting DI's
presence stand in for UDL's absence: treating "I differentiate constantly" as evidence
that barriers have been designed out, when high differentiation volume is closer to the
opposite signal: it is evidence of how many barriers the design still has.

**Be fair to the blur.** CAST itself cautions that the two frameworks "intersect and can
work together, though they represent different theoretical frameworks and implementation
strategies": it does not claim they are cleanly separable moment to moment in a real
classroom. Its own account of the relationship, once it turns from definition to
practice, softens: a UDL-designed lesson with strong built-in flexibility can still call
for differentiation the design didn't anticipate, and responding well to that need is
part of how the next redesign gets found. Hold the distinction as real and useful: it
is not a technicality. But treat "purely proactive" as something a lesson approaches
through iteration, not a state a single lesson is simply in from day one.

## In practice

Run this instead of the label check:

1. **When an adjustment happens, ask which side of the line it's on.** Did you change
   something about the lesson's design (materials, methods, goal-framing, assessment
   options) that will still be there next time you teach it? Or did you change what one
   student experiences, this one time? The first is UDL; the second is DI.
2. **Keep a barrier log, not just a fix log.** When DI happens, write down what barrier
   it was responding to. Barriers that recur across students or across sections are a
   redesign signal: the input to [[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]] for a lesson already
   built, or [[design-a-lesson-with-udl]] for one not yet built.
3. **Use CAST's own sequence to convert the pattern into design.** The four-step
   process (set the goal, anticipate barriers, design flexible options, align
   assessment) is explicitly iterative: teach, gather what came up, redesign. A barrier
   log is the raw material that feeds step two next cycle.

**Worked pass.** A teacher models a word-problem procedure; a handful of students lose
the steps and need to be walked through it individually. Handled as DI: re-teach those
students one-on-one, this week. Nothing wrong with that as a first response. Handled as
UDL, once the pattern repeats across two units: build a graphic organizer highlighting
the steps, a short process video, and manipulatives that make the steps visible:
available to everyone, every time, from the start. The second version doesn't replace
the first; it reduces how often the first is needed, which is CAST's own stated
mechanism connecting strong Tier 1 design to fewer downstream interventions.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **DI is not something UDL is trying to eliminate.** Even a well-designed UDL lesson
  will still need differentiation for needs the design didn't anticipate: see
  [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]] for the related and more consequential
  version of this error, assuming good design retires individualized support entirely.
- **The line is not always clean in the moment.** Planning and responding are often
  interleaved in real teaching rather than strictly sequential; use the distinction as a
  diagnostic lens applied after the fact, not a rule you force every second of teaching
  through in real time.
- **Volume of differentiation is a signal, not neutral labor.** A class that requires
  constant individual adjustment on the same barrier is telling you something about the
  design, not just about the students.
- **This is a design-discipline trap, not an evidence trap.** It doesn't turn on whether
  UDL "works": see [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] for that separate question.
- **DI drift often precedes rigor drift.** Patching the same barrier for one student
  over and over, under UDL's name, tends to be the first step toward quietly lowering
  what the task requires rather than only how it's reached. See
  [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]] for the construct-shift version of this
  same slide.
