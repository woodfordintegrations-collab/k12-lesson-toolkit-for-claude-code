# Choice Is Not the Point

## Summary

A choice board is easy to build and easy to photograph for a walkthrough, and neither of
those is evidence of UDL. [[optimize-choice-and-autonomy]] (7.1) already covers what
choice is for and how much to offer; this page covers the specific way choice gets
faked. The trap: presenting several options as if they were meaningfully different paths
to the goal, when every option actually routes through the same underlying capability.
CAST's own guardrail on this consideration is direct: "It is therefore not enough to
simply provide choice."

## When to reach for it

You are at risk of this trap right when you're building a choice set and haven't checked
what each option actually demands of the learner underneath its surface format. The
tell, stated precisely: **choices that all require the same underlying capability.**
Three ways to present a report (essay, poster, slideshow) that all require fluent
writing are one option wearing three hats, not three options.

| Signal | What's actually happening |
|---|---|
| You built a choice board and stopped there | check whether the barrier that actually blocked anyone is still standing |
| Every option demands the same skill to execute (usually writing) | one option in three costumes: the trap this page names |
| A specific learner still can't complete any of the options | not a choice problem at all: an access problem; see [[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] or [[access-to-assistive-technologies]] |
| The options genuinely differ in what they demand (write it, say it, build it) | real 7.1: no trap here |
| The options differ, but so does what's being assessed | you may have drifted into [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]] |

## How it works

Choice boards are tempting for reasons that have nothing to do with whether they work.
They are fast to make, they look identical to what an observation checklist rewards
(flexible methods and materials, visible options, student voice), and they match the
single most common shorthand for UDL in circulation. Loui Lord Nelson names the
shorthand directly:

> "While choice is a popular way to talk about UDL, and every guideline begins with
> 'Provide options for,' when the conversation ends there, no one benefits — UDL exists
> to help instructional designers plan lessons and environments that foster learner
> agency."

The trap is not that choice is fake UDL: 7.1 is real, CAST endorses it, and every
consideration in the Guidelines carries its own Design Options section offering
concrete strategies (the "Provide options for" framing Loui Lord Nelson's quote above
reflects is 2.2-era language; 3.0 renamed it to "Design Options," though the
underlying habit of always supplying options is unchanged). The trap is stopping
at the surface of a genuine instruction. CAST names six dimensions a choice can vary:
content, tools, rewards, practice or assessment opportunities, layout and graphics, and
sequence or timing. A choice board that only ever touches layout and graphics (same
content, same tool, same skill, different color scheme) is technically "choice" and
does nothing for the learner whose actual barrier sits somewhere else entirely.

The report example is the cleanest case. "Present your findings as an essay, a poster,
or a slideshow" looks like three options. Ask what each one actually requires a learner
to produce: an essay requires composing coherent written prose. A poster requires
captions and labels: written prose again, just shorter, with formatting overhead added
on top. A slideshow requires bullet text and, usually, a script: written prose a third
time. A learner whose barrier is written composition has exactly zero working options
among the three, dressed as three.

## In practice

**The de-hatting test.** For every option in a choice set, write down the underlying
capability a learner needs to already have in order to use it: not the format, the
skill underneath the format. If two or more options list the same capability, you have
one option, not two, no matter how different they look on a handout.

**Ask it from the blocked learner's seat, not the design's.** "If a student who cannot
[do the shared capability] tried each option, could they succeed at any of them?" If the
answer is no across the board, the choice set has not touched the barrier: it has
relabeled it.

**Fix by opening a different dimension, not by adding more of the same one.** For the
report example, response mode is the dimension that actually varies the underlying
capability: a recorded oral summary, a live interview-style conversation, a diagram with
spoken narration. These require explaining and organizing findings, the actual
construct, without requiring written composition to get there. Compare this to
[[multiple-media-for-communication]] (5.1), the consideration that names this move on
the representation side.

**Trace it back to the barrier audit, not to a menu.** CAST's own design sequence puts
barrier identification before option design: name what's actually stopping learners
first, then build options that target that named barrier. A choice set assembled from a
generic menu of "ways students like to work," with no barrier behind it, is decoration
regardless of how many options it has. See [[audit-a-lesson-for-barriers]].

## Gotchas & constraints

- **Not every choice board is this trap.** A set that genuinely varies the underlying
  capability required (write it, say it, build it) is exactly what 7.1 asks for.
  Don't let this page turn into reflexive suspicion of all choice; the diagnostic is the
  de-hatting test, not a blanket rule against choice boards.
- **Fixing this trap can slide into the next one.** If, in opening a genuinely different
  capability, you also change what's being assessed (grading a recorded summary on
  delivery charisma instead of the argument's structure), you've traded this trap for
  [[lowering-rigor-in-the-name-of-flexibility]]. The construct has to survive the fix.
- **CAST does not give a checklist for "genuinely different capability."** Judging
  whether two options share a bottleneck is a design call, not a mechanical test: the
  de-hatting test above is this page's own operationalization, not something CAST
  states.
- **Evidence caveat.** As [[optimize-choice-and-autonomy]] notes, engagement effects for
  choice specifically are not established with any precision in the literature; nothing
  here claims that de-hatted options measurably outperform hatted ones. See
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]].
