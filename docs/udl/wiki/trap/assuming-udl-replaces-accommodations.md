# UDL Does Not Retire the IEP

## Summary

The trap: reading good universal design as grounds to withdraw, decline to write, or
informally stop honoring an individualized accommodation. IDEA and Section 504
obligations attach to the student and run through a legal process; they are not
discharged by improving Tier 1 design, no matter how well that design is done. This
page exists because the underlying inference (better design, less need for
accommodation) is *directionally true*, which is exactly what makes the illegal
version of it tempting.

## When to reach for it

You are at risk of this error when you notice yourself moving from "this design
choice will help most learners, including some who currently need accommodation X"
to "therefore the student who needs accommodation X no longer needs it," without an
IEP or 504 team, a reevaluation, or current data on that specific student's
performance in the room.

Concrete tells:

- An IEP team member says "the general ed classroom already uses UDL, so this
  student doesn't need extended time / a scribe / audio text anymore," decided in
  the hallway, not at a team meeting.
- A curriculum designer feels the accommodations budget or paperwork is now
  redundant because the lesson offers flexible options.
- A district evaluates whether UDL professional development lets it reduce
  accommodation staffing or supports as a cost measure.
- A teacher quietly stops providing a written accommodation because "everyone gets
  that option now anyway."

Routing table, the actual problem, and where to go instead:

| The actual situation | Where to go instead |
|---|---|
| You want your design to reduce how often accommodations are *needed* | This is a legitimate UDL goal, not the trap: see [[proactive-design-vs-retrofit]] |
| You're deciding whether a design move is an accommodation or a construct-changing modification | [[construct-irrelevant-variance]] / [[design-an-accessible-assessment]] |
| You're tempted to skip writing or stop honoring an accommodation because "UDL covers it" | This is the trap: the answer below is no, not without the IEP/504 team |
| You're asking whether your UDL-designed material is *legally* accessible | [[mistaking-udl-for-accessibility-compliance]] |

## How it works

The inference is tempting because it follows from something CAST itself says and
that is empirically plausible: barriers live in the design, not the student, and a
better-designed Tier 1 genuinely can reduce the number of students who need
individual accommodations. Your staged implementation source states this directly:
"proactive integration of Universal Design for Learning (UDL) principles can
substantially diminish the necessity for individual accommodations," and the
mechanism is intuitive: fewer construct-irrelevant barriers upfront means fewer gaps
an accommodation has to patch.

The trap is the next step, which nothing in CAST's own language licenses. Report
CAST's actual position precisely, because it is more modest than practitioners often
assume: **CAST and NCEO say UDL reduces the need for accommodations, not that it
eliminates it.** Your staged sources are explicit on both sides of this:

> "It's important to note that UDL does not replace the need for individual
> accommodations, though in real-world situations, people sometimes have competing
> access needs."

> "Universally designed assessments will not eliminate the need for all
> accommodations. However, they may reduce the need for them."

So the strong claim, "good UDL design means this student no longer needs their
accommodation," is not a CAST claim being followed too literally. It is an
inference practitioners add on their own, past what the source supports.

**Why it is wrong, legally.** IDEA does not mention UDL. A comprehensive search of
IDEA's statutory text and its implementing regulations at 34 CFR Part 300 finds no
explicit reference to UDL at all. IDEA's mechanism for individualization is the IEP
process (Free Appropriate Public Education, individualized goals, related services,
Least Restrictive Environment), and that mechanism has its own procedural
safeguards: evaluation, team decision-making, parent/guardian involvement. Nothing
in a design framework can substitute for that process or amend an IEP by default.
Where UDL does appear federally, in ESSA, the mandate is narrower than people assume:
states must use UDL principles in state assessment design "to the extent
practicable" (§1111(b)(2)(B)(xiii)) and "to the extent feasible" for alternate
assessments (§1111(b)(2)(D)(iv)): both qualified requirements about *assessment
design*, with no enforcement mechanism specified, and neither one reaches classroom
instruction or curriculum design, let alone accommodation practice. An IEP is an
enforceable legal document; a design framework (doubly so a qualified, assessment-
scoped one) cannot amend it.

**Why it is wrong, empirically.** Disability-studies scholarship treats the
elimination claim as a live harm, not just a procedural slip:

> "One concerning aspect of UDL may be the stigmatization and erasure that may occur
> through the concept and phrase, as the potential failure to specifically and
> categorically meet the individual needs of disabled students is alarming.
> Scholars have claimed that by reducing barriers and impediments, individual
> students would no longer need to self-identify as disabled or require
> accommodations, as they would inherently be available. However, without critical
> discussions, practices risk being ineffective or even unknowingly harmful to
> disabled people."

And directly on the empirical question of whether UDL can stand in for individualized
support:

> "The research suggests that UDL cannot entirely replace individualized support.
> Accommodations must be selected based on individual student needs and there is no
> 'one size fits all' solution."

## In practice

- **Never let a Tier 1 redesign be the sole grounds for removing or declining an
  accommodation.** Removal or modification of an IEP or 504 plan runs through the
  team process defined by IDEA's procedural safeguards (evaluation, current data,
  team decision, guardian involvement), not through a curriculum designer's or
  single teacher's judgment call.
- **Treat a stronger Tier 1 as information for the team, not a decision.** If a new
  UDL-designed unit appears to make an existing accommodation redundant, bring
  current performance data to the next IEP/504 review. Let the team decide whether
  the barrier is actually resolved for that student.
- **Watch for surface overlap that isn't real overlap.** A whole-class choice in
  timing (7.1) is not automatically equivalent to a student's individually
  calibrated extended-time accommodation: the conditions, amount, and triggering
  mechanism usually differ even when the label sounds the same.
- **Ask what the accommodation was compensating for.** If it was patching a
  construct-irrelevant barrier that better design has genuinely closed, that is a
  case to bring to the team. If it addresses something specific to the student that
  design cannot reach, it stays regardless of how good Tier 1 gets.
- **Document, don't assume.** If an accommodation goes unused because a UDL option
  covers the same ground, write that down as evidence for the next review: it is
  not itself the review.

**A worked pass.** A student has an IEP accommodation for audio versions of
assigned text, tied to a documented reading disability. The teacher adopts
[[multiple-ways-to-perceive-information]] (1.2) and now offers audio for the whole
class. Does the IEP accommodation lapse? No. Until the IEP team formally reviews the
change with current data and revises the IEP, the accommodation stands exactly as
written. The teacher can and should flag the design change for the next review, but
cannot let the paperwork go informally unenforced in the meantime.

## Gotchas & constraints

- CAST's and NCEO's own language is consistently "reduce," never "eliminate." If a
  page, a PD deck, or a colleague states the strong claim as CAST doctrine, that
  claim is not sourced: flag it.
- This is the same trap shape as [[choice-as-a-substitute-for-design]]: a genuine,
  good-faith design move gets treated as sufficient on its own, when the source's
  own qualifying language says it is not.
- ESSA's UDL language is scoped to assessment design and is explicitly qualified
  ("to the extent practicable" / "to the extent feasible"). It provides no legal
  basis for instructional or accommodation decisions, and it specifies no
  enforcement mechanism even within its own scope.
- The disability-studies critique here is not only a compliance risk: it is framed
  as an erasure risk to disabled students' identity and self-advocacy, which is a
  stronger claim than a documentation gap.
- Overall evidence for UDL's effectiveness is thin and contested (Murphy, 2021,
  found "no rigorous published research has demonstrated any improvement in an
  education intervention designed with UDL principles"), so even the modest
  "reduces the need for accommodations" claim rests on weaker footing than
  confident phrasing usually implies. See [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]].
- NIMAS, a separate IDEA-based technical standard for accessible-format instructional
  materials, is unaffected by any of this: schools must comply with it regardless
  of UDL adoption.
