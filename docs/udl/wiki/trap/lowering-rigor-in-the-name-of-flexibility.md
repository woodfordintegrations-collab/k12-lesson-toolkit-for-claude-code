# Flexibility Is Not Lowered Expectations

## Summary

This is the trap that does the most damage to UDL's credibility, because it is committed
by well-meaning people using UDL's own vocabulary. The slide: "vary the means" (real
UDL) quietly becomes "vary the goal" (construct-shift, not UDL). CAST's own text names
the guardrail directly: "All learners need to be challenged, but not always in the same
way," and measurement theory gives that guardrail a precise test: whether the redesign
changed *how* a learner reaches the goal, or changed *what counts* as reaching it.

## When to reach for it

You are at risk of this trap whenever a learner can't currently meet a goal as phrased,
and your response changes what "meeting the goal" means for them rather than only how
they get there. The tell: after the redesign, would this learner's result and a peer's
result be measuring the same thing? If not, you modified the task, not accommodated the
learner, whatever you're calling it.

Concrete signals you're already inside the trap:

- You reduced item difficulty, cut required content, or narrowed scope, and are
  describing it as "flexibility" or "meeting them where they are."
- A learner's score rose noticeably after the change, and the reason is that the task
  got easier: not that a barrier unrelated to the goal got removed.
- Asked what unchanged construct the original task and the redesigned task both measure,
  you can't answer.

| The actual situation | Where this fits |
|---|---|
| Varying how mastery is shown; the construct is unchanged | real UDL: [[firm-goals-flexible-means]] |
| Reducing what counts as mastery for everyone, in the name of flexibility | this trap |
| An individually-documented, IEP-team decision to reduce expectations for one student | a modification made through the proper individual process, different from, and not licensed by, a general design move; see [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]] |
| You genuinely can't tell which of the above you're doing | run the test in **In practice**, below |

## How it works

"Vary the means, never the goal" is [[firm-goals-flexible-means]]: the framework's
central operating move, and [[optimize-choice-and-autonomy]] already leans on it. The
trap happens because that instruction licenses touching almost everything about *how* a
task is done: format, pacing, tools, response mode, support. Once a teacher is fluent in
touching all of that, the next step feels continuous rather than like a boundary
crossing: if changing *how* something is presented helps, changing *what's expected*
must help more. It doesn't. It reverses in kind partway along that path, and nothing
about the framework gives a felt signal at the crossing. Loosening the means and
lowering the goal feel like the same generous impulse from the inside of the decision.

They also look identical from the struggling learner's seat in the moment: both reduce
friction right now. Only a construct-validity lens tells them apart, and most classroom
teachers were never trained in that lens. This is a documented, not speculative, gap:
research on distinguishing accommodations from modifications found that special
educators and administrators scored significantly higher than general-education and
elective teachers on making the distinction correctly: a finding that "has held
consistent across subsequent studies."

**CAST's own text is where the guardrail lives, and part of it can be misread in the
trap's favor.** Consideration 8.2 states: "All learners need to be challenged, but not
always in the same way. It is important for the learning environment to embed a variety
of tools, resources, and supports to promote successful engagement with the learning
goal... productive struggle is a key component of an engaging and rigorous learning
experience." Its design directives include "offer options with varying modes of
complexity or difficulty." Read in isolation, that phrase sounds like license to lower
the floor. Read against the sentence it follows, "not always in the same way," it is
not licensing a lower goal; it is licensing different *paths* to the same challenge, with
productive struggle named explicitly as something to preserve, not remove. The ambiguity
is real, and it is exactly the sentence a rigor-lowering redesign will point to in its
own defense.

**Assessment theory makes the distinction precise where CAST's prose leaves room to read
it either way.** Accommodations change presentation format, response mode, setting, or
timing without altering what's measured; scores stay comparable. Modifications change
task difficulty, content, or scope; they measure something else, and scores on the
modified and standard forms are not meaningfully comparable. Beddow (2011) gives this an
empirical tell rather than a purely definitional one: modifications were found to raise
average scores across both students with and without IEPs by altering the cognitive
demand of items: not simply removing barriers to access. Score elevation of that kind
signals construct shift, not barrier removal.

## In practice

Run this test on any redesign that touches what's expected of a struggling learner:

1. **Extend the option to everyone.** If this same change were offered to the whole
   class, would the assessment still measure the same thing it measured before? If yes,
   it's accommodation-shaped: fine as a universal design move. If no, it's
   modification-shaped, and universal design is the wrong lever for it.
2. **Ask what moved: the barrier, or the task.** Did the change remove something
   irrelevant to the construct (reading demand on a math item, handwriting on a content
   quiz, time pressure where speed isn't the point), or did it reduce what the
   construct itself requires (fewer steps, simpler numbers, less content)?
3. **Watch what happens to the score, not just to the student's affect.** A real
   accommodation should not move the score distribution much, because it was only ever
   addressing noise around the construct. A large score jump is evidence the *task*
   changed, not evidence the design finally reached the learner.

**Worked pass.** A standard reads "solve a two-step word problem involving fraction
operations." The construct is reasoning with fractions across two steps. Real UDL: read
the problem aloud, provide a fraction manipulative, accept any response modality: none
of that touches fraction reasoning, so the redesign is accommodation-shaped no matter how
many supports stack up. The trap: replacing it with a one-step whole-number problem
because a student "isn't ready yet." That doesn't vary the means of reaching the goal:
it swaps the goal for an easier one, and the resulting score can no longer be read
against the original standard at all. If a genuine, individual reduction in the
construct is warranted for a specific student, that's a decision an IEP team makes
deliberately and documents: not something a teacher backs into by "differentiating" the
floor for a whole class. See [[apply-udl-to-a-standard]] for reading a standard this way
before designing against it, and [[design-an-accessible-assessment]] for applying the
same test to assessment design specifically.

## Gotchas & constraints

- **This is a design-discipline argument, not an empirical-outcomes argument.** It rests
  on measurement theory, the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* and
  Messick's validity framework, not on a UDL effect size.
  [[does-udl-improve-student-outcomes]] covers the separate question of whether
  UDL-designed instruction improves achievement; nothing here depends on that
  literature.
- **CAST does not give a bright-line test for every case.** Some redesigns are
  genuinely ambiguous: removing a strict time limit is construct-irrelevant for most
  content assessments, but construct-relevant if fluency or automaticity is specifically
  what's being measured. The test in **In practice** is a diagnostic, not a mechanical
  rule that resolves every edge case.
- **Individually-documented modifications are not this trap.** An IEP team deciding,
  deliberately and on the record, to reduce expectations for one student is a different
  and legitimate process: not a general design move a teacher makes by default. See
  [[assuming-udl-replaces-accommodations]] for the adjacent error of assuming good
  design retires that process entirely.
- **The trap runs in both directions.** Refusing any adjustment to a struggling
  learner's task by invoking "rigor," when the adjustment was genuinely
  accommodation-shaped, is not caution: it's the same conflation from the other side,
  mistaking barrier removal for standard-lowering when it wasn't.
- **This is the trap that costs UDL its credibility with skeptics of the framework**,
  because it is usually committed in UDL's name rather than by ignoring UDL:
  offered here as this wiki's own judgment on top of the source, not a cited finding.
- **The slide often starts as DI drift.** Constant reactive patching of the same
  barrier for one student, mistaken for UDL rather than logged as a redesign signal,
  is frequently the first step toward quietly lowering what's expected rather than
  only how it's reached. See [[confusing-udl-with-differentiated-instruction]].
