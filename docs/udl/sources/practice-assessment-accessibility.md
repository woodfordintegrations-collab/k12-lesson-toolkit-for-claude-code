# UDL in Assessment and Technical Accessibility: Research Synthesis

## 1. CONSTRUCT-IRRELEVANT VARIANCE: Definition and Measurement Theory

### Precise Definition

**Construct-irrelevant variance (CIV)** is the introduction of extraneous, uncontrolled variables into measurement that cloud the assessment's ability to measure the focal construct. When CIV is present, variance in test scores reflects factors unrelated to what the test is designed to measure, adversely affecting both the meaningfulness of score inferences and the validity associated with those inferences.

Sources of CIV include but are not limited to: reading comprehension demands irrelevant to the content being assessed, ability to perform under time pressure, writing ability (when not the construct), handwriting legibility, visual acuity, auditory processing speed, and anxiety in test-taking environments.

### Citation

**Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing** (2014), developed jointly by the American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, and National Council on Measurement in Education. The 2014 revision explicitly addresses "Minimizing Construct-Irrelevant Components Through Test Design and Testing Adaptations" ([AERA Standards](https://www.aera.net/publications/books/standards-for-educational-psychological-testing-2014-edition)).

**Messick's Unified Validity Framework** (1995) provides the theoretical foundation underlying the 2014 Standards. Messick identified six distinguishable aspects of construct validity: content, substantive, structural, generalizability, external, and consequential. The consequential aspect explicitly encompasses fairness and bias concerns—including sources of CIV—that threaten score validity across populations ([Messick framework reference](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/j.2333-8504.1994.tb01618.x)).

### Connection to UDL and Assessment

UDL theory positions construct-irrelevant variance as a **legitimate target for elimination through universal design**. When an assessment includes reading demands, timed constraints, or format barriers that are not essential to what is being measured, these become design flaws—not accommodations needs. CAST guidance states: "Universally designed assessments identify and eliminate unintended and/or irrelevant barriers in the measurement itself" ([CAST Assessment Resources](https://cast.org/resources/udl-schoolwide-implementation-criteria)). This represents a measurement-first approach: CIV removal is not "lowering standards" but rather improving the validity and fairness of the measurement.

## 2. THE DISTINCTION: Changing HOW a Construct Is Measured vs. Changing WHAT Is Measured

### Framework

**Accommodations and modifications differ in their impact on construct validity:**

- **Accommodations** remove barriers to access without altering the construct being measured. They change *how* a student demonstrates knowledge of the construct (presentation format, response mode, setting, timing) while keeping the construct itself constant. Valid accommodations maintain construct relevance and allow meaningful comparison of scores.

- **Modifications** alter what the assessment is designed to measure. They change the *task itself*—reducing item difficulty, simplifying content, or narrowing the scope of what students must demonstrate. Modifications produce scores on different constructs and typically render score comparisons invalid across modified vs. standard forms.

### Research Evidence

[Beddow (2011)](https://nceo.info/references/article-journal/11951) empirically demonstrated that modifications increase average scores across both students with and without IEPs by altering the cognitive demand of items, not simply removing barriers. This score elevation signals construct shift rather than barrier removal.

[NCEO guidance](https://nceo.info/Assessments/general_assessment/accessibility-and-accommodations) states: "The purpose of an accommodation is to remove disadvantages due to conditions that are irrelevant to the construct the test is intended to measure without giving unfair advantage to those being accommodated."

### Measurement Implication

The measurement principle here is critical: a valid accommodation should not change score distributions significantly if it truly addresses only construct-irrelevant variance. If an accommodation causes substantial score increases, it likely introduced construct modification rather than access.

## 3. ACCESSIBLE ASSESSMENT DESIGN: Universal Design Principles Applied to Measurement

### Seven Core Elements

[NCEO identifies](https://nceo.info/Assessments/universal_design) seven foundational components for universally designed assessments that integrate accessibility into the measurement itself:

1. **Inclusive assessment population**: Design for the full range of intended test-takers from the outset, not as an afterthought.
2. **Precisely defined constructs**: Clarity about what is being measured reduces construct-irrelevant demands and helps eliminate unnecessary complexity.
3. **Accessible, non-biased items**: Items avoid construct-irrelevant barriers such as unnecessarily complex language, visual ambiguity, or cultural assumptions.
4. **Amenable to accommodations**: Design permits meaningful accommodations (e.g., braille format, speech-to-text response) without compromising the construct.
5. **Simple, clear, and intuitive instructions and procedures**: Administrative and procedural clarity itself reduces variance from test-taking confusion.
6. **Maximum readability and comprehensibility**: Prose minimizes cognitive load unrelated to the construct.
7. **Maximum legibility**: Visual presentation (contrast, font, spacing) removes barriers for readers with low vision.

### Implementation Pathway

NCEO provides a nine-step iterative approach:
Plan → Define purpose → Require design in contracts → Address design during development → Include expertise in reviews → Test usability → Conduct tryouts → Analyze results → Monitor and revise.

### Key Principle

Universal design balances accessibility with measurement validity. Simplifying language or diversifying response formats must not inadvertently alter the construct. The goal is construct preservation with barrier elimination.

## 4. ACCOMMODATIONS VS. MODIFICATIONS: Standard Distinction in Assessment

### Formal Definition

From [NCEO](https://nceo.info/Assessments/general_assessment/accessibility-and-accommodations) and [California Department of Education](https://www.cde.ca.gov/sp/se/sr/promoretntn.asp):

**Accommodations** are changes in testing procedures, presentation format, response mode, or setting that do *not* alter what the test measures. Accommodations are designed to provide equitable access to students whose disabilities or conditions create barriers to demonstrating their knowledge of the focal construct.

**Modifications** are substantive changes to the content, cognitive demand, or scope of what is measured. Modifications result in assessment of different constructs and typically prevent meaningful score comparison with the standard form.

### Five Accommodation Categories

[NCEO research](https://nceo.info/references/thesis/11026) identifies five standard categories:

1. **Setting**: Separate testing location, individual administration, small group.
2. **Timing/Scheduling**: Extended time, frequent breaks, alternative testing windows.
3. **Presentation**: Questions read aloud (live, recorded, or synthesized speech); font enlargement; braille format; simplified formatting.
4. **Response**: Student dictates to scribe; uses word processor instead of handwriting; uses speech-to-text; uses alternative keyboard.
5. **Other**: Bilingual glossaries, mathematics formula sheets (when not the construct).

### Teacher Knowledge Gap

[Cisar (2004)](https://nceo.info/references/thesis/11026) found that special educators and administrators scored significantly higher than general education and elective teachers in distinguishing accommodations from modifications and in their appropriate use—a finding that has held consistent across subsequent studies. This gap reflects the need for professional development on measurement principles.

## 5. UDL AND STANDARDIZED/SUMMATIVE ACCOUNTABILITY TESTING: Tensions and Constraints

### The Core Tension

Standardized accountability assessments are historically shaped by administrative constraints: inexpensive delivery, standardization across conditions, and cost-efficient scoring. These constraints have produced multiple-choice, paper-and-pencil formats with linear item selection and simple scoring algorithms. UDL principles—flexibility, multiple means of action and expression, diverse formats—often conflict directly with these standardization requirements.

### Specific Constraints

[CAST guidance](https://udloncampus.cast.org/page/assessment_udl) and [research literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.02905) identify these persistent tensions:

- **Format rigidity**: Standardized tests use recognition and recall (low cognitive demand but easy to score) rather than sophisticated cognitive tasks (harder to develop and prohibitively expensive to score at scale).
- **Timing constraints**: Universal test administration windows prevent flexible scheduling accommodations that would otherwise reduce time pressure as a construct-irrelevant barrier.
- **Scoring algorithms**: Accountability systems rely on automated, simplistic scoring (often correct/incorrect only), which cannot accommodate the complex, multidimensional response formats that UDL would recommend.
- **Comparability demands**: High-stakes testing requires vertically comparable scores across cohorts and horizontally comparable scores across demographically different groups—a demand that pressures test developers toward homogeneous, standardized item sets rather than diverse, flexible assessments.

### No Elimination, But Reduction

[NCEO research](https://nceo.info/Assessments/universal_design) confirms that "universally designed assessments will not eliminate the need for all accommodations. However, they may reduce the need for them." Within the constraints of large-scale accountability testing, universal design can reduce—but not eliminate—construct-irrelevant barriers.

### The Honest Assessment

States are required to develop student academic assessments using universal design for learning principles "to the extent practicable" while maintaining measurement validity and providing alternate assessments for students with significant cognitive disabilities. This formulation acknowledges that full UDL flexibility and large-scale standardized accountability testing represent conflicting objectives, not perfectly compatible goals.

## 6. TECHNICAL ACCESSIBILITY STANDARDS: WCAG, Section 508, and EPUB

### WCAG 2.2 (Current Standard)

**Current Version**: Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2, published October 5, 2023 (updated December 12, 2024). ([W3C WCAG Overview](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/))

**Conformance Levels**: Three levels of conformance are defined (from minimum to maximum rigor):
- **Level A**: Web page satisfies all Level A success criteria, or a conforming alternate version is provided.
- **Level AA**: Web page satisfies all Level A and Level AA success criteria, or a Level AA conforming alternate version is provided.
- **Level AAA**: Web page satisfies all Level A, Level AA, and Level AAA success criteria, or a Level AAA conforming alternate version is provided.

Most organizations target Level AA as a standard of practice. ([W3C Understanding Conformance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/conformance))

**WCAG Scope**: WCAG is a technical standard with 86 testable success criteria across four principles (Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust). Conformance is binary—either a success criterion is met or it is not—and can be automatically tested or manually verified against explicit criteria.

### Section 508 Standards

[Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act](https://www.section508.gov/manage/understand-scope-technical-requirements/) is U.S. federal legislation requiring electronic information and communication technology procured, maintained, or used by federal agencies to be accessible. Section 508 standards align electronic document accessibility requirements to WCAG 2.0 Level AA, with four specific exceptions.

**Electronic documents** (including PDFs, Word documents, Excel spreadsheets, etc.) must meet these standards. Assessment materials delivered digitally—whether formative classroom assessments or high-stakes accountability tests—are covered under Section 508 requirements if used in federal programs or by federally funded institutions.

### EPUB Accessibility Standard

[EPUB Accessibility 1.1](https://www.w3.org/TR/epub-a11y-11/) (current version; EPUB Accessibility 1.2 in progress) is the W3C standard for accessible digital publishing. EPUB publications build on open web technologies (HTML, CSS, JavaScript, SVG) and must conform to WCAG 2.0 Level AA to be considered accessible ([EPUB A11y Standard](https://www.w3.org/TR/epub-a11y-11/)).

**Relevance to Assessment**: Assessment materials increasingly distributed as digital publications (interactive practice tests, online assessments, learning management system content) must meet EPUB accessibility standards. This includes semantic markup for reading order, image alternative text, video captions, proper heading hierarchy, and compatibility with screen readers.

### Key Distinction: WCAG Is Technical, UDL Is Pedagogical

This is critical: WCAG conformance (meeting specific technical success criteria) is **necessary but not sufficient** for UDL-aligned assessment design.
- WCAG ensures that assistive technology (screen readers, magnification, voice control) *can access* digital content.
- UDL ensures that assessment *design itself*—the pedagogical choices about what and how to measure—provides multiple means of engagement, representation, and expression.

A high-stakes accountability test might technically conform to WCAG Level AA while remaining pedagogically misaligned with UDL (e.g., multiple-choice only format, no alternative response options, high time pressure). Conversely, a pedagogically rich UDL assessment might have technical accessibility gaps (e.g., images without alt text) that reduce its actual usability by screen reader users.

## 7. ASSISTIVE TECHNOLOGY AND UDL: Complementary but Distinct

### What Assistive Technology Does

[CAST guidance](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/action-expression/interaction/assistive-technologies/) defines assistive technology (AT) as tools designed to address specific barriers learners with disabilities face when interacting with materials. Examples include:
- **Text-to-speech**: Renders written text as synthesized or natural speech.
- **Screen readers**: Convey digital text and images as synthesized speech or braille output.
- **Speech-to-text**: Converts spoken words to text for input.
- **Magnification software**: Enlarges display elements.
- **Switch devices**: Allow learners with motor disabilities to navigate using single-switch activation.
- **Voice command systems**: Enable learners to control devices through speech.

### UDL's Perspective on AT

UDL principles propose that **excellent universal design should reduce AT dependence**. [CAST states](https://www.openaccess-ca.org/post/synergy-of-udl-aem-and-at/): "UDL stresses the best possible design, resulting in little or no need for AT accommodations."

This reflects a design hierarchy: *design for accessibility first*, then provide AT tools as a safety net for cases where perfect universal design is impossible or insufficient. UDL's goal is to shift from an AT-centric remediation model (fix the barrier after the fact) to a design-centric prevention model (build access into the assessment from the start).

### The Failure Mode: Technically Accessible, But AT-Unusable

A critical problem identified across research: **universally designed materials can still be inaccessible to screen readers if technical implementation is poor.** Examples:

- Dynamic user interfaces with visual meaning not conveyed through accessible markup or ARIA (Accessible Rich Internet Applications) semantics.
- Complex data visualizations represented only as images, without accessible alternatives.
- Interactive response formats (drag-and-drop, drawing tools) with no keyboard or voice-control fallback.
- Dynamically loaded content that screen readers cannot detect or announce.

([Research on AT accessibility failures](https://www.marshall.edu/online/digital-accessibility-and-universal-design-for-learning/); [CAST technical guidance](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/action-expression/interaction/assistive-technologies/))

### Implementation Principle

Effective use of AT in assessment requires both:
1. **Universal design** of the assessment task itself (what is being measured, how response options are structured).
2. **Technical accessibility** of the delivery platform (WCAG conformance, native HTML semantics, ARIA roles where needed, keyboard navigation, screen reader testing).

Neither alone is sufficient. A beautifully designed, pedagogically rich assessment that fails keyboard navigation or screen reader testing will exclude learners who depend on AT. Conversely, a technically perfect WCAG-AA-compliant interface that presents only multiple-choice questions offers no additional expression options to learners who might benefit from written, spoken, or constructed response alternatives.

## 8. AI AND GENERATIVE TOOLS IN ASSESSMENT DESIGN AND MATERIALS: Emerging Guidance

### Current Research Status

**Confidence Level: UNCONFIRMED.** There is no authoritative guidance from standards bodies (AERA, APA, NCME, CAST, NCEO) specifically addressing generative AI in assessment design or materials accessibility as of August 2026. The sources below represent emerging practice research and university-level experimentation, not yet consolidated into professional standards or evidence-based policy.

### What Research Suggests

1. **AI as an Accessibility Tool**: When given explicit, operationalized accessibility instructions through carefully engineered prompts, generative AI tools can improve material accessibility. Primary use case: generation of alternative text and long image descriptions for complex diagrams.
   ([University of Pittsburgh TILT resource](https://teaching.pitt.edu/resources/using-generative-ai-to-create-more-accessible-student-learning-experiences/); [SDSU Accessibility office](https://accessibility.sdsu.edu/tools-and-evaluations/ai-accessibility))

2. **Remediation Pipelines**: Educators can use generative AI to audit and suggest revisions to course materials and assessments for UDL alignment, incorporating structured remediation feedback to improve accessibility of human-authored content.

3. **Assessment Authenticity and AI Training Data**: A measurement concern exists regarding assessments that depend on course-specific materials or resources postdating AI training data. If assessments rely on material not available in public training corpora, this creates a barrier specific to AI-assisted test-taking that differs from traditional test-taking challenges. ([Research on resource accessibility](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02046))

4. **Technical Accessibility of AI-Generated Content**: AI-generated content often fails technical accessibility standards without explicit remediation. One research protocol ([Chen et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00749)) evaluated AI-generated materials against WCAG-derived criteria and found that unconfigured generative AI produces accessible text but often inaccessible images, diagrams, and interaction patterns.

### What Remains Unknown

- Whether AI-generated assessments maintain measurement validity (i.e., whether construct-irrelevant variance is introduced or reduced).
- Best practices for using AI to design accommodations vs. modifications in assessment.
- Ethical frameworks for AI-assisted high-stakes testing or score calibration.
- Whether AI can reliably detect construct-irrelevant variance in assessment items.

### Professional Recommendation

Until authoritative professional guidance emerges, institutions should:
1. Treat AI-generated assessment materials with the same rigor as human-authored materials: validation, pilot testing, technical accessibility audit.
2. Disclose AI generation in assessment contexts where transparency is required (e.g., high-stakes testing, published research).
3. Maintain human expert review of AI-generated assessment materials for measurement validity.

## Summary: Key Takeaways for Assessment Practice

1. **Construct-irrelevant variance is a measurement problem, not an accommodation problem.** Universal design eliminates CIV through better initial design, not through test-time accommodations alone.

2. **The HOW/WHAT distinction is the measurement foundation for distinguishing accommodations from modifications.** Valid accommodations preserve the construct; modifications change it.

3. **Accessible assessment design is a systematic discipline** with seven core elements (NCEO) and integrates construct clarity, item design, and procedural simplicity.

4. **WCAG technical conformance and UDL pedagogical design are distinct and complementary.** Both are necessary; neither is sufficient alone.

5. **Assistive technology fills the gap where universal design is incomplete,** but excellent design should minimize AT dependence rather than presume it.

6. **Standardized accountability testing and UDL flexibility create genuine tensions** that cannot be fully resolved. Implementation requires pragmatic trade-offs, not false harmony.

7. **AI in assessment remains emerging practice without authoritative guidance.** Current use should be treated as experimental and subject to the same validity audits as any new assessment method.
